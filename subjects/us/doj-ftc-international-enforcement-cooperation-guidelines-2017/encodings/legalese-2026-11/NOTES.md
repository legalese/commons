# DOJ/FTC Antitrust Guidelines for International Enforcement and Cooperation (2017): notes for a reviewer

**AGENCY ENFORCEMENT POLICY, NOT LAW.**
These guidelines say how the Department of Justice and the Federal Trade Commission will exercise their enforcement discretion in matters with an international dimension.
They do not say what a court must hold, and nothing in this encoding does either.
Every result names what the agencies (or the Department, or the FSIA, or a statute as the 2017 text summarises it) say, with the sentence's own modal; none says lawful, unlawful, illegal or violates.

**"No independent rights or obligations."**
The coordinator's common brief for this batch (`BRIEF-COMMON.md`, item 6) says the guideline creates "no independent rights or obligations".
Those words are **not in this text**: a search of the extraction for "independent rights" finds nothing.
This text's own disclaimers are these, and the header comment of `intl-nouns.l4` carries the first two:

- 1 [pdf page 7]: the guidelines "are not intended to, do not, and may not be relied upon to create any rights, substantive or procedural, enforceable at law by any party in any matter, civil or criminal".
- 1 [pdf page 6]: "No set of guidelines could possibly indicate how the Agencies will assess the particular facts of every case"; n.5: the outcome "depends on the specific facts and circumstances of the case".
- 1 [pdf page 6]: users "should rely on qualified counsel", and those wanting more specific statements of enforcement intentions should use the Department's Business Review procedure or the Commission's Advisory Opinion procedure (n.7, n.8).

The result types carry the disclaimer by being named for what a sentence of the text says (`What section 4.2.2 says of foreign sovereign compulsion`), never for a legal status of the conduct.

## 2. Seat

The text says whom it is written for: "updated guidance to businesses engaged in international activities" (1 [pdf page 4]), who "should rely on qualified counsel" (1 [pdf page 6]).
So the seat is a business or person engaged in international activities, with counsel, asking what the agencies say they will do with its conduct; for chapter 5, also the person who produced information to the agencies, asking what the agencies say they will do with it.
It is never an official: no result tells the agencies what to do, and every result reports what the agencies say of themselves.
There is no separate seat layer (no `@export` functions written for the seat); as in the IP row, that is deferred.

**Reported, never weighed**: the comity considerations of 4.1; the statements against the defense of 4.2.2; the limits of 2.8 and 2.9; the standards of 2.9 not shown.

**Inputs, never computed**: every finding the text leaves to the agencies or a court.
The FTAIA findings are three-valued (`MAYBE BOOLEAN`: not yet determined, found to hold, found not to hold; F8).
Whether the conduct is "commercial", whether a statute applies, whether a measure is a command, whether petitioning is a sham, whether a remedy is needed or consistent with comity, and the rest are booleans or enums the caller supplies, each named as the finding it is (lesson 5 of the brief).

**Answers NOTHING** where the text states no outcome.
Every NOTHING branch in the rule modules has a boundary test that reaches it (section 8, "NOTHING probes").
There are no numeric inputs, so the brief's case of an impossible input (a share outside 0 to 100) does not arise; inputs that contradict each other are read in a stated order (F29).

**Not encoded**: the statutes' prohibitions and penalties as such (2.1, 2.3, 2.5, 2.10, 2.11; penalties in 2.1 and 2.4); dated figures (HSR thresholds, n.32; the 2016 penalty limit, n.38; ACPERA's sunset, n.41); foreign law (n.55, n.172); statutes the text only mentions (n.30, n.59); chapter 1; 5.1.2; the descriptive parts of 5, 5.1.3 and 5.2; Example H; Annex 1.
