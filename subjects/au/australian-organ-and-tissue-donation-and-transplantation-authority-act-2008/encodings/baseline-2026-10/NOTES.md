## Seat

**The registering donor.** The question is "Is my registration recorded, and what does
it do?".

The Act is addressed to the Authority, its CEO, DonateLife Agencies and grant
recipients. Nothing in it is addressed to a donor. What it means for the donor is two
things: what may be published about them, and with whose consent; and the plain
statement that it leaves consent to removal to State law. So the decisions are phrased
as the donor would ask them:

- `Is my registration made under this Act`
- `What does this Act say my registration does`
- `Does this Act change the consent needed to remove my organs`
- `Is keeping my registration within the Authority's functions`
- `Whose consent lets the Authority publish information identifying me`
- `May the Authority publish information identifying me`
- `Is this person one of my authorised family members`
- `May my family member publish information identifying me`
- `May a DonateLife Agency publish information identifying me`

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone.** Followed. No regulations were retrieved. The people the regulations
  may add are asked as facts.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the Authority, the CEO, DonateLife Agencies,
  grant recipients, authorised family members, and a parent or legal guardian.
- **Judgments handed to others are answered conditionally.** Followed. These are all
  asked as facts:
  - whether another law or a Minister's instrument confers a function (s 11(1)(l), (m));
  - whether a function falls within a constitutional head (s 11(2));
  - a person's legal capacity (s 58(2)(ac), (ad));
  - the constitutional means of publication (s 58A(2)(e), (3)(b));
  - whether a body is a declared DonateLife Agency (s 5F).

## Why this scope

The single event cites "the Register", and the plan entry points to s 11. The Act does
not mention the Register. So the baseline encodes:

- that absence;
- the saving of State consent law (s 59(2));
- the one function a register could rest on (s 11);
- the provisions that do speak to a donor: confidentiality, and who consents to
  identification (s 58); and, after death, who the family are and who may tell the
  donor's story (ss 5, 5A, 58A).

That is everything in the Act a donor meets. The rest is the Authority's constitution.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **The Act never mentions the Australian Organ Donor Register.** In the deposit,
   "Register" appears only for the Federal Register of Legislation. The Act does not
   establish the Register, give anyone power to keep it, or say what registering does.
   Whatever law sets up the Register, it is not in this deposit, and this encoding does
   not say what that law is. Asserted.
2. **The Act leaves consent to removal entirely to State and Territory law and the
   common law** (s 59(2)), "whether living or deceased". So whether Chloe's registration
   is consent, and whether donation is opt-in or presumed, is answered by the Human
   Tissue and Transplant Act 1982 (WA), not by this Act. Asserted.
3. **Keeping a registration could be an Authority function, but the Act does not say it
   is.** It could fall under s 11(1)(d), "collect ... information relating to" the
   identification of potential donors (s 4, para (b)). That is a **reading**. It could
   also be conferred by another law or a Minister's instrument (s 11(1)(l), (m)). Either
   way it is limited to the s 11(2) heads of power. Who actually keeps the Register is
   asked as a fact. Asserted, on those inputs.
4. **The donor's consent controls identification, from 18, and for some from 16**
   (s 58(2)).
   - At 18 or over, the donor consents.
   - Under 16, a parent or legal guardian consents.
   - At 16 or 17, the donor consents if they have legal capacity, and otherwise a parent
     or legal guardian does.
   - After death, any one authorised family member consents, or, if there is none, an
     individual the regulations authorise.

   It is a **reading** that paragraphs (a) to (ad) speak of a living person. Asserted.
5. **No consent lets the Authority reveal which recipient received the donor's organ**
   (s 58(3)). Not the donor's own consent, and not the family's. Asserted.
6. **"Authorised family member" has no age limit, and it starts only on death** (s 5).
   - Chloe's 14-year-old brother Jake qualifies. Under the WA Act, by contrast, a
     brother must be 18 to be next of kin.
   - A former spouse does not qualify. Of several people who would qualify as partner,
     only the last does (s 5A(2)).
   - Kin under Aboriginal or Torres Strait Islander kinship rules qualify only where the
     donor has traditional kinship ties.

   Asserted.
7. **After death, the family may tell the donor's story** (s 58A(3)). It must be for an
   educational, commemorative, promotional or community-awareness activity of the
   Authority or a DonateLife Agency, made by one of the listed constitutional means. A
   DonateLife Agency or grant recipient may do the same, but only with an authorised
   family member's consent (s 58A(2)). Both need an actual donation and transplantation
   (s 58A(1)). Asserted.
8. **The Act gives the donor nothing to decide about donation itself.** The only choice
   it gives the living donor is consent to being identified (s 58(2)). Everything else
   is the Authority's machinery, or is left to State law. Asserted (findings 2 and 4).
