trade mark applicant and registered owner, with the design defaults ruled on 2026-10-18. No REFUSE. No pipeline, no
coverage table, no independent test pass, no human gate. Not for public use as advice.

**Edition:** Compilation No. 48, compilation date 19 September 2026 (includes amendments up to Act No. 87, 2026), register

## Seat

**The founder, as trade mark applicant and registered owner.** In the WA lives the applicant is Daniel's company, Jarrah
Legal Technologies Pty Ltd, and Daniel speaks for it. The Act applies registration, renewal, infringement relief and removal
to the applicant and owner. It is the owner who asks "how long does my registration last, and is the copy an infringement?"
(the plan's question). The Registrar's and the court's powers are encoded as what they mean for the owner. Decisions are
phrased that way: `Will my application be accepted`, `When does my registration first expire`, `What happens if I ask to
renew on`, `Is the copy an infringement`, `Is my letter safe from an unjustified threats claim`, `Can my mark be removed
for non-use`, `May I mark it as registered`.

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers or dates.** Followed. The plan names no regulations and none
  are deposited. The prescribed renewal window and the forbidden signs are inputs. Fees are left out, because no decision
  the events ask turns on a fee.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the applicant, the registered owner, an authorised user, the Registrar, the
  court, the other person who uses a sign, and the person aggrieved by a threat.
- **A judgment handed to someone else answered conditionally.** Followed. These are all inputs: how far a mark is
  inherently adapted to distinguish, and the s 41(4) combined-effect finding (the Registrar); "deceptively similar" (s 10)
  and "similar goods" (s 14); the Registrar's satisfaction under s 44(3) and (4); whether the other person establishes no
  likelihood of confusion (s 120(2)); "well known" (s 120(3)); the s 122(1)(f) opinion; and the s 101(3) discretion.
- **The Act's own definitions worked out; borrowed terms asked.** Followed. "Sign" (s 6), "trade mark" (s 17), "filing
  date" and "date of registration" (ss 6, 72), "priority date" (s 12) and the s 6A month rule are worked out. "Legal
  personality" and "good faith" are asked.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** Followed: one registration (`Registration`), one application (`Applicant`,
  `Mark`, `Rival`), and one use of a sign by another person (`OtherUse`).
- **The Act's own terms, with a plain gloss.** Followed.
- **Operative sections in; machinery out.** Followed, and narrowed further because the Act is large (see below).

## The lives

- **Daniel, d046 (1 September 2031, the JARRAH mark applied for).** The decisions are `Is my sign a trade mark`, `May I
  apply` and `Will my application be accepted` (accepted, on the input that JARRAH is sufficiently adapted to distinguish
  legal software). If another person had filed JARRA earlier for similar services, the application would be rejected under
  s 44, or might be accepted with conditions under s 44(3) or (4). The registration takes effect on 1 September 2031 and
  first expires on 1 September 2041. Renewal is due under Division 2. A late renewal is possible until 1 March 2042.
  Renewal carries the registration to 1 September 2051. The mark may not be shown as registered before its particulars are
  entered (s 151). The scenario gives only the filing date. The entry date (15 April 2032) and first use (15 June 2031) are
  hypothetical, and so is the 12-month renewal window.
- **Daniel, d056 (11 February 2039, a competitor trades as JARRA; a letter; they rebrand).** On that day the registration
  is in force. `Is the copy an infringement` gives an infringement under s 120(1) for the same services, on the input that
  JARRA is deceptively similar. It falls under s 120(2) for closely related goods, unless the competitor shows no likelihood
  of confusion. There is no infringement under a s 122 own-name defence, or under s 124 continuous use from before Jarrah's
  first use. `Can I sue for this use` and `Can I get damages or an account of profits` both answer yes; s 127 would take
  damages away. `Is my letter safe from an unjustified threats claim` is yes when the acts infringe (s 129(4)), and yes for
  a mere notification (s 130A). The competitor's counter-move, a non-use application, fails because JARRAH is in use.

## Why this scope

The plan names ss 72 and 120. The prompt asked for the founder's questions as applicant and owner: what can be registered,
how long registration lasts and how to renew, the exclusive rights and infringement as far as the events need, and removal
for non-use. The letter in d056 brings in ss 129 and 130A. Displaying "registered" brings in s 151. Left out: the
application and opposition procedure, amendment and rectification, Division 3 renewal (the encoding says when it applies,
then stops), assignment, s 121 notices, exhaustion (s 122A), customs seizure, the offences other than s 151, collective,
certification and defensive marks, the Madrid Protocol, and the courts, Registrar and attorneys machinery.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **Registration runs from the filing date, not the date it is granted.** s 72(1) makes registration effective "from (and
   including) the filing date", and s 20(3) dates the exclusive rights from then. So once JARRAH is entered in 2032, its
   rights reach back to 1 September 2031. Asserted (the date the registration takes effect, and the term).
2. **The 10 years count from filing, even with a Convention date.** s 72(3), Note 1: a registration backdated to an
   overseas application still expires 10 years after the Australian filing date. Asserted for the backdated effective date;
   the expiry is the same rule.
3. **Renewal is always 10 years from the old expiry, early or late.** ss 77 and 79. A late renewal within 6 months is
   allowed as of right, but s 128(1) bars any action for infringements done in the gap. Asserted.
4. **Months follow s 6A, and the encoding reads the 10 years as 120 months.** The Act's own example (30 September plus
   3 months is 30 December) holds. Reading 10 years as 120 months is an inference; it only matters for a filing on
   29 February. Asserted (the 29 February case gives 28 February 2042).
5. **A tie on priority dates goes against me.** s 44(1)(b) rejects if my priority date "is not earlier than" the other's,
   so filing on the same day is not enough. s 44(4) continuous earlier use saves the application. Asserted.
6. **Infringement turns on the court's view of "deceptively similar".** s 120(1) needs no proof of confusion for the
   registered goods or services. s 120(2) lets the other person show there is no likelihood of confusion. s 120(3) reaches
   unrelated goods only for a well-known mark. Asserted, with "deceptively similar" as an input.
7. **Prior use beats registration only from before the earlier of my filing and my first use.** s 124(1)(e), (f). A JARRA
   used from July 2031 is not protected, because Jarrah's (hypothetical) first use in June 2031 came first. Asserted.
8. **A letter that only notifies is never a threat. A threat is safe only if the acts really infringe.** ss 130A, 129(4).
   Asserted.
9. **Non-use removal needs 3 years from entry, and 3 years of registration ending a month before the application.**
   ss 92(4)(b), 93(2). Use by an authorised user counts (s 7(3)), and the owner carries the burden of showing use (s 100).
   An obstacle to use (s 100(3)(c)) or the s 101(3) discretion can keep the mark. Asserted.
10. **Marking a mark "registered" before it is entered is an offence.** s 151(1), (5), with a maximum of 60 penalty units.
    Asserted.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- The Trade Marks Regulations 1995 should be deposited, so that the renewal window, forbidden signs, classes and fees are
  read rather than asked.
- Division 3 renewal (ss 80A-80H), opposition (Part 5), and the s 122A exhaustion defence are not encoded.
- These readings are inferences that case law or the Acts Interpretation Act might settle: the 10 years read as 120
  months; the expiry day itself counted as outside the registration; "within 6 months after" read as including its last
  day; and the arithmetic used for the 3 years ending one month before.
- `Is my registration in force on` answers yes for a day before entry once the mark is entered (s 72(1) backdating). That
  is not the same as asking, on that earlier day, whether the mark was then on the Register.
- The Australian Consumer Law s 18 point in d056 belongs to the Competition and Consumer Act encoding, not this one.
