## Seat

**The worker** — here the night security officer employed by a security contractor and posted to an office tower,
who falls on a wet stairwell on patrol. The Act imposes its duties on businesses, officers and workers, but the
person it protects, and the one it is applied to when something goes wrong at work, is the worker. Every decision is
a question the worker would ask: `Am I a worker`, `Does this person owe me the primary duty of care`, `Does this person
owe me the workplace duty`, `What must be done next about this risk`, `Is my injury or illness serious`, `Must this
business notify the regulator`, `May I cease or refuse this work`, `Was it discriminatory conduct for a prohibited
reason`, `Can the Tribunal make an order for me`, `Does the Act give me a right to sue`. Provisions addressed only to
a duty holder, the regulator, an inspector, a court or the Tribunal (ss 30A-33, 38, 39, 110, 112) are encoded as what
they mean for the worker: what the person who failed me can be fined, who must notify and how long the record is kept,
what may be done to the site, what I may ask the Tribunal for and by when. The worker's own duty (s 28) and its
Category 3 fine are encoded too, since the worker is also a duty holder.

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers.** Followed: every fine is in dollars in the Act. The
  deposited General Regulations are brought in where they define a notifiable incident (reg 699 prescribes serious
  illnesses for s 36) and where they fill in what the s 17 duty requires (regs 35, 36). No regulation was found
  prescribing a further dangerous incident (s 37(l)) or an excluded kind of injury or incident.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the worker, the person conducting a business or undertaking, the person
  with management or control of a workplace, the officer, other persons at the workplace, the health and safety
  representative, the regulator, the inspector, the court, the Tribunal. The building manager in the cases is an
  illustrative PCBU the scenarios do not name.
- **A judgment handed to someone else answered conditionally.** Followed: "so far as is reasonably practicable"
  (s 18), the medical practitioner's opinion (s 36(1)(e)), the knowledge and disregard for industrial manslaughter
  (s 30A), whether the accused or defendant proves the reason was not dominant or substantial (ss 110(2), 113(2)) and
  the s 113(3) defence are inputs.
- **The Act's own definitions worked out, borrowed terms asked.** Followed: worker (s 7), PCBU (s 5, including
  "volunteer association"), workplace (s 8), person with management or control (s 20(1)), officer (s 4A, with the
  Corporations Act s 9 meaning asked), serious harm (s 30), notifiable incident, serious injury or illness and
  dangerous incident (ss 35-37), discriminatory conduct and prohibited reason (ss 105, 106) are worked out. "Business
  or undertaking", "contract of service", "reasonable concern" are asked.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** Followed: one worker and one incident or one issue; a duty is asked
  person by person, so the same facts can be put for the employer, the tenant and the building manager in turn.
- **The Act's own terms with a plain gloss.** Followed, in comments and in the `@desc` of every input.
- **Operative sections in; machinery out.** Followed: the regulator, inspectors and their notices, the Commission and
  the Tribunal's constitution, review of decisions, regulation-making powers and the consequential amendments are
  out. Departure: some operative parts are also out for size (see Why this scope).

## The lives

- **Daniel d044** (1 June 2031): Jarrah's first three employees, asked from an employee's seat. *Does my employer owe
  me the primary duty of care? Must its directors exercise due diligence? Must I be consulted? May I be charged for
  safety gear?* Yes; yes (a Corporations Act s 9 officer); yes, where I am directly affected and it is reasonably
  practicable; no (s 273).
- **Daniel d080** (5 October 2053): the night officer's fall, from the tenant's side, asked from Jake's seat. *Whose
  duty?* Sentinel's primary duty (it engaged him). The tenant owes him the primary duty only if it caused him to be
  engaged (contracted Sentinel) or directs his work; it owes him the s 20 workplace duty only if it manages or
  controls the stairwell in whole or part; it always owes the s 19(2) duty not to put him at risk by its own work. The
  building manager who controls the common areas owes the s 20 duty. The scenarios do not say which; both answers are
  asserted.
- **Jake k085** (5 October 2053): the fall, eleven weeks off, 14% whole person impairment. *Is it a notifiable
  incident? Who must notify? What can Sentinel be fined? Does this Act let me sue?* Notifiable if a doctor thought it
  likely to keep him off normal work for 10 days or more (the WA s 36(1)(e) limb) — not otherwise, on these facts,
  and not as a dangerous incident; Sentinel must notify immediately and keep the record five years; a company whose
  failure caused serious harm faces up to $3 500 000; this Act gives him no civil action (s 267), so the 15% threshold
  under the Workers Compensation and Injury Management Act 2023 is untouched by it.

## Why this scope

The seat asks who owed the worker a duty, what it required, what happens after an incident, the right to stop work,
and protection for raising a safety issue. That is Part 2 Divisions 1-5 (without the upstream duties of designers,
manufacturers, importers, suppliers and installers, which reach a worker only through plant), Part 3, s 47 of Part 5,
the Part 5 Division 6 cease-work rules, Part 6, and the three general rules of Part 14 that change what a worker can
do. Left out for size: health and safety representatives' elections and powers other than the s 85 direction,
committees, issue resolution, provisional improvement notices, the regulator's and inspectors' powers, WHS
undertakings, review, prosecutions, and every hazard-specific Part of the regulations. Reg 78 (falls from one level to
another) was read and left out: a slip on a stair is the general duty and reg 40, not reg 78.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **The duty follows control, not the contract.** Sentinel owes the primary duty because it engaged the officer; a
   tenant that neither engaged nor directs him owes him the primary duty only if it "caused [him] to be engaged",
   which a tenant that contracted the security company arguably did. Asserted both ways.
2. **A tenant owes the s 20 workplace duty only "to the extent" it manages or controls the place.** Whether a tenant
   controls a common stairwell is a fact the Act does not settle. Asserted both ways.
3. **Everyone running a business owes everyone else s 19(2).** A tenant whose own cleaners wet the stair owes the
   officer a duty not to put him at risk, whoever controls the stair. Asserted.
4. **WA adds a 10-day limb to "serious injury or illness".** s 36(1)(e): an injury that "in the opinion of a medical
   practitioner, is likely to prevent the person from being able to do the person's normal work for at least 10 days"
   is notifiable. The officer's eleven weeks off makes his fall notifiable on a doctor's certificate even if he was
   never an inpatient. Asserted.
5. **A worker slipping is not a dangerous incident.** s 37(f) covers "the fall or release from a height of any plant,
   substance or thing", not a person's fall. Asserted.
6. **Reg 699 makes the listed zoonoses serious illnesses only when caught in work with animals**, but any infection
   work significantly contributed to is one. The zoonosis limb is asserted; the general infection limb is encoded
   but not asserted.
7. **Industrial manslaughter reaches only the PCBU and its officers.** A worker whose failure kills someone faces the
   Category 1 maximum for "another individual", $340 000 and 5 years. Asserted.
8. **The Act gives a worker no civil action for a breach of the safety duties.** s 267 excepts only Part 6
   (discrimination and coercion). Asserted.
9. **Discrimination: different tests in the criminal and the civil route.** The offence needs the prohibited reason to
   be the "dominant" reason (s 104(2)), presumed unless the accused proves otherwise (s 110(2)); the Tribunal needs a
   "substantial" reason (s 112(4)), presumed likewise (s 113(2)), with a defence of reasonable conduct to comply with
   the Act (s 113(3)), and the application must be within 1 year. A failed workers' compensation claim on the same
   matter does not bar the Tribunal (s 115(c)). Asserted.
10. **Stopping work protects continuity of engagement unless the worker unreasonably refused suitable, safe
    alternative work** (s 88). The Act does not say that refusing breaks continuity; it only withdraws the protection.
    Asserted.
11. **A health and safety representative must consult the business and try to resolve the issue before directing a
    stop, unless the risk is too serious and immediate** (s 85(2), (3)), and cannot direct one at all without the
    prescribed initial training (s 85(6)). The consultation rule is asserted; the training rule is encoded but not
    asserted.
12. **s 5(8) is permissive.** A strata company responsible only for residential common areas "may be taken not to be"
    a PCBU; the encoding leaves strata companies out rather than guess how that "may" is exercised. Not asserted.
