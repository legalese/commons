# Magistrates Court Act 2004 (WA) — baseline encoding

## Method

**Method: baseline.** Written straight from the deposited text with the
`writing-l4-rules` skill, from the seat of the individual to whom the law is
applied, with the design defaults ruled on 2026-10-18. No `REFUSE`. Checked with
the stable `l4` of VS Code extension 1.6.98. No pipeline, no coverage table, no
independent test pass, no human gate. Not for public use as advice.

There was no earlier encoding of this Act.

Every question is published with `@export` and a plain-words description, and
every input carries an `@desc`, so the module can be deployed to Legalese Cloud as
it stands.

## Edition

- **The Act:** the Magistrates Court Act 2004 (No. 47 of 2004), compilation as
  deposited at `../../registers/source-bundle/mrdoc_47672.txt`
  (legislation.wa.gov.au mrdoc 47672, retrieved 7 September 2026). Its s 11A
  carries No. 10 of 2023.
- **The numbers** used are in the Act itself: 21 days to appeal a registrar's
  decision (s 29(2)); a contempt fine of up to $12 000 or up to 12 months'
  imprisonment (s 16(4)).
- **"Written law"** in ss 7, 9 and 14 is read with the Interpretation Act 1984
  s 5, as deposited in `../../../interpretation-act-1984/registers/source-bundle/`:
  "all Acts for the time being in force and all subsidiary legislation", "Act"
  meaning an Act of the Western Australian Parliament.
- **No regulations or rules of court.** Neither the Magistrates Court Regulations
  nor the rules of court are deposited. So two things are inputs: whether JPs may
  constitute the Court for a step (s 7(2)), and what the rules delegate to a
  registrar (s 28(1)).

## Seat

**The claimant.** That person asks:

- "May the Magistrates Court hear my claim, and under which law?"
- "Who will hear it — a magistrate, or JPs? Where?"
- "Do the ordinary rules of evidence apply?"
- "If the registry refuses my claim, or a registrar decides against me, what can I
  do?"
- "What can I see of the Court's record of my case?"

Almost all of this Act speaks to the Court, its magistrates, registrars and the
Chief Magistrate. Each such rule is encoded as what it means for the claimant.

Each 2026-10-18 default:

- **The Act alone, plus the instruments that set its numbers.** Followed. The
  numbers are in the Act. The regulations and rules are not deposited, so the two
  points that turn on them are inputs.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the claimant (a party), the Court, a
  magistrate, JPs, a registrar, the Chief Magistrate.
- **Judgments handed to others are asked as inputs.** Followed:
  - whether a magistrate gives leave to lodge a refused document (s 17(6));
  - whether a magistrate extends the time to appeal a registrar (s 29(3));
  - the Chief Magistrate's approval of a sitting outside the State (s 8(1)(c));
  - whether the conferring law reaches the claim (ss 9, 10). That is answered by
    the other Act, not guessed here.
- **The Act's own definitions worked out from facts.** Followed for the Court
  officers (s 3) as glosses. The borrowed term "written law" is taken from the
  Interpretation Act 1984 s 5.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** One case in the Court (s 3, "case").
- **The Act's terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed in comments.
- **Operative sections in, machinery out.** Followed, and this Act is mostly
  machinery. Left out:
  - the appointment, tenure and removal of magistrates (ss 5, 6, Schedules 1-3);
  - administration (Part 4), except ss 28 and 29, because they reach a party;
  - practice directions, rules of court, regulations and fees (ss 38-42).

## The split with the Magistrates Court (Civil Proceedings) Act 2004

This Act constitutes the Court and sends the reader elsewhere for its
jurisdiction. Section 9 gives it "the jurisdiction conferred on it by this Act and
by other written laws". Section 10 says its civil jurisdiction "is set out in the
Magistrates Court (Civil Proceedings) Act 2004".

So the division between the two baselines is:

- **The Civil Proceedings Act baseline**
  (`../../../magistrates-court-civil-proceedings-act-2004/encodings/baseline-2026-10/`)
  has the money limits ($10 000 and $75 000), the claims the Court cannot hear,
  the consumer/trader claim, the minor cases procedure, lawyers and costs, and
  the appeal to the District Court.
- **This baseline** has who sits, where, which law the jurisdiction comes from,
  the rules of evidence, refused documents, registrars, the self-represented
  party and the record.

The joint is one input. `that law gives the Court jurisdiction over my claim` is,
for an ordinary civil claim, the answer to the other baseline's
`Can the Magistrates Court hear my claim`. Nothing is computed twice.

## The lives

- **Jake, k062 (10 March 2036, aged 29): the Fair Work Ombudsman, then a small
  claim in the Magistrates Court; judgment for $4,100.** Thirteen months of
  overtime at ordinary time from Sentinel under the Security Services Industry
  Award. The scenario cites the Fair Work Act 2009 s 548, and this Act for
  "jurisdiction". Two things are assumptions: that he sued in the WA Magistrates
  Court sitting in Perth, and that he represented himself. The questions:
  - `May the Magistrates Court hear my claim`: yes, under the Commonwealth law
    that confers it, which s 9 does not itself name;
  - `Who may constitute the Court for my case`: one magistrate;
  - `May the Court sit there for my case`: yes;
  - `Do the Supreme Court rules of evidence apply to my case`: no. The Fair Work
    Act s 548(3) says that in small claims proceedings "the court is not bound by
    any rules of evidence and procedure";
  - `Must the Court explain cross-examination to me`: yes;
  - `Can I get this from the record of my case`, for the written judgment: yes,
    as of right on request.

  Whether he is owed the $4,100, and what the court may order, is the Fair Work
  Act's question (ss 545, 548), not this Act's.

## Why this scope

The life cites "jurisdiction" and no section. The seat's first question, "may the
Magistrates Court hear my claim", needs ss 9 and 10, and they hand it on. The rest
of the scope is what this Act itself says that reaches a claimant in a hearing:

- who may hear the case and how a bench of JPs decides (s 7);
- where it may sit (s 8);
- the rules of evidence (s 14);
- a claim refused at the registry (s 17);
- slips in the judgment (s 23);
- what a registrar may not decide, and the appeal from one (ss 28, 29);
- the duty to a self-represented party (s 30);
- the record (s 33);
- the most a party or witness can suffer for contempt (s 16(4)).

The brief asked for a small baseline (25-35 assertions); this has 34. Left out:

- criminal jurisdiction (ss 11, 11A), which is not the claimant's;
- what counts as a contempt and how it is dealt with (s 15, s 16 save (4));
- oaths, affidavits, warrants, cancellation and inspection (ss 18-22);
- reasons for judgment, the effect of process, presumptions, review orders and
  immunities (ss 31, 32, 34-37). These are noted below.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **This Act gives the Court no civil jurisdiction of its own.** Section 10 says
   only where it "is set out": the Civil Proceedings Act. A claim with no law
   behind it gets "no". So does a claim that the conferring law does not reach.
   Asserted.
2. **Section 9 does not name Commonwealth jurisdiction.** It speaks of "other
   written laws". Under the Interpretation Act 1984 s 5 that means WA Acts and
   subsidiary legislation. Jake's claim reaches the Court through the Fair Work
   Act s 548, a Commonwealth law. **Inference, not read in the deposit:** a
   Commonwealth law of that kind confers jurisdiction on a State court of its own
   force. So the answer is yes, but not because of s 9, and the encoding says so.
   Asserted.
3. **Jake's claim would not stay in the Civil Proceedings Act's minor cases
   procedure.** That Act's s 28(2) says the Court "must order" the general
   procedure where a case "involves any jurisdiction conferred on the Court by a
   law of the Commonwealth". Jake's small claim runs under the Fair Work Act
   s 548 instead. Noted, not asserted here: it belongs to the other two Acts.
4. **One magistrate is the rule.** JPs sit only "in circumstances prescribed by
   the regulations or by another written law" (s 7(2)). A requirement in another
   written law displaces both, except for an adjournment or another procedural
   decision (s 7(4)). Asserted.
5. **An evenly divided bench of JPs adjourns.** Nobody has a casting vote. The
   JPs choose the place and date; if they cannot agree, the one first appointed
   chooses. All of them must be present throughout (s 7(6)). Asserted.
6. **A registrar can never enter final judgment after a trial, or find anyone
   guilty of contempt** (s 28(1)(b), (c)). Any other delegated decision can be
   appealed to a magistrate within 21 days. A magistrate may extend that "even if
   it has elapsed", and the appeal is a new hearing (s 29). Asserted.
7. **A refused claim lodged in time is saved.** If the registry refuses a claim as
   frivolous, vexatious or an abuse of process, a magistrate may give leave to
   lodge it. If it was first lodged within its time limit, it may then be lodged
   "even if the time limit has by then expired" (s 17(7)). If it was first lodged
   late, s 17(7) does not help. Asserted.
8. **The Supreme Court's rules of evidence apply unless another law displaces
   them** (s 14). Section 14 says "Subject to any other written law". Reading the
   Fair Work Act s 548(3), a Commonwealth law, as displacing them is an inference.
   Asserted, with the displacement as an input.
9. **A self-represented party must be told the rule about putting contradicting
   evidence to the other side's witnesses, and what happens if they do not**
   (s 30). It is the Court's duty, not the party's, to raise it. (The rule is
   known in the common law as the rule in Browne v Dunn; that name is not in the
   Act.) Asserted.
10. **A party gets some records as of right.** These are the lodged and issued
    documents, the evidence, written judgments and orders, and:
    - the transcript, only "if an electronic recording has been made ... and a
      transcript of it has been prepared";
    - the magistrate's own record of proceedings, only "if no such recording was
      made".

    Everything else needs leave (s 33(3), (4)). A non-party gets leave unless the
    Court "has good reason not to" (s 33(8)). The magistrate's private notes are
    never required (s 33(11)(a)). Asserted.
11. **Contempt can cost up to $12 000, up to 12 months' imprisonment, or both**
    (s 16(4)). A witness who ignores a summons "without reasonable excuse" is in
    contempt (s 15(2)). If the fine is unpaid, the Court may order imprisonment
    until it is paid or for up to 12 months, whichever is shorter (s 16(5)). The
    fine is asserted; the rest is not.
12. **The Court is presumed to have been properly constituted and to have had
    jurisdiction, "in the absence of proof to the contrary"** (s 34(1), (7)). A
    party who says otherwise must prove it. Not asserted.
13. **No prerogative writ lies against a magistrate or registrar** (s 35). A
    person aggrieved applies instead to the Supreme Court for a "review order"
    (s 36). That application may be redirected as an appeal under the Civil
    Proceedings Act and sent to the District Court (s 36(5)(b)). Not asserted.
