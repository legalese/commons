## Seat

**The public patient**: "What does the public hospital charge me?"
The Act and the Order fix what a WA health service provider may charge, and the person charged is the patient (s 57(1): "payable by or on behalf of the person who received the health service").
The patient is classified, elects public or private, owes the fee, and has a compensable charge recovered from their compensation. The Minister, the Department CEO, the provider and its chief executive act on that person.
Every exported question is the patient's: "Is my care a public health service?", "Is the hospital a public hospital?", "May the hospital charge me at all?", "What class of patient am I?", "What does the hospital charge me per day?", "What will my stay cost?", "What do I pay for an out-patient visit, a medicine, an MRI, a pathology test, or to stay with a patient?", "Is the amount consistent with the Order?", "How much do I owe after a waiver?", "Can the hospital take its charge out of my compensation?", "Must the hospital have a complaints procedure?", "May my information be disclosed?".
The Order's charges address the provider. Here they are encoded as what the patient can be charged.

## Why this scope

The plan entry names Part 6 (fees and charges, ss 55-57), with Part 4 cited by the facts, and the deposited Fees and Charges Order. The brief asks what the public hospital may charge a Medicare-eligible public patient, a private patient and an ineligible person, and what patient rights the Act itself states.
That is what is encoded, with the definitions each answer needs:

- ss 7 and 8 (whether this is the public system at all);
- s 55 and the Order's classes and charges;
- s 57 and the compensable-charge sections (Part 6 as amended in 2023);
- the only patient-facing duties the Act states: s 34(2)(f) (complaints) and ss 217, 219 and 220 (information).

Left out because no life asks for them and each is self-contained: specialised orthoses and surgically implanted prostheses (Order cll 9, 10, Division 6); s 57B (services of former public hospitals before 1 July 2016); the s 58 regulations on recovery, apportionment and review; s 59 (charges for things that are not health services); parking fees under Part 16.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **The Act gives no right to free public hospital care in terms. The "nothing to pay" comes from the Order.** Section 5 says the public hospital system "is based on the Medicare principles set out in the National Health Agreement". Section 55(1) lets a health service provider "determine and impose a fee or charge" for any health service. The only exceptions are a service the National Health Agreement says is not to be charged (while it is in force) and one covered by a s 56(2)(b) order. The free stay for a Medicare-eligible public in-patient is the Order's Schedule 1 Division 1 item 1(a), "no charge". It is a fixed fee of nothing, not a right stated in the Act. Asserted.
2. **This closes the gap the Health Insurance Act baseline left for k090.** That baseline found that Medicare pays nothing for public-hospital care (HIA s 17(1)(a), s 19(2)) and that s 128C protects public patients only for childbirth. Jake's free three weeks come from the WA Order's "no charge" for public in-patients, read with s 55(1)(a) and the Agreement (not deposited). Asserted (the Order's part).
3. **Public or private is the patient's election, and it changes the bill.** An eligible in-patient "must elect" on admission (cl 12(4)). A public in-patient pays nothing. A private in-patient pays $467 a day in a shared ward and "no more than $774" in a single-bed ward "if taken at patient's request" (item 1(b)). The single-room figure is a ceiling, not a fixed fee. A private same day patient pays $385 a day (item 6(b)). Asserted.
4. **Not being Medicare-eligible costs $3,417 a day as an in-patient, $3,258 as a same day patient and $397 per out-patient service.** Medicines are charged at cost (Sch 1 Div 1 items 1(e), 6(d), 4(b); cl 10A). Eligibility is borrowed from the Health Insurance Act 1973 s 3 (Order cl 3(1)). Asserted.
5. **Two groups of Medicare-ineligible people pay nothing at all.** These are asylum seekers awaiting a protection or safe haven visa, and involuntary mental health patients treated under the Mental Health Act 2014 (cl 6A, "Despite any other provision of this Part"). They need not even be classified (cl 11(2)). An involuntary patient's other treatment is charged as usual. Asserted.
6. **A Medicare-ineligible person with a compensation claim is a compensable in-patient but not a compensable same day patient.** Clause 3A(4)(a) requires a compensable same day patient to be "an eligible person". The in-, day and out-patient limbs do not. So the same visitor pays $3,529 a day admitted overnight (as compensable) and $3,258 a day admitted and discharged the same day (as ineligible). Asserted (the classes).
7. **Third-party motor vehicle patients are charged nothing for a hospital bed; other compensable patients pay $3,529 to $7,202 a day.** Division 2 items 2, 3 and 10(a), (b) say "no charge" wherever the Motor Vehicle (Third Party Insurance) Act 1943 "applies or prima facie appears to apply". Workers compensation and damages claimants pay $3,529 a day, $4,305 for 24-hour airway care, $7,202 ventilator-dependent, and $4,465 a day as same day patients. Reading: the motor vehicle "no charge" covers ventilator and airway cases too; a nursing home bed ($385) is not excepted. Asserted, except the nursing home bed.
8. **Nothing fixes the charge for a Medicare-ineligible or compensable day patient.** Schedule 1 has an item only for "eligible day patients" (no charge), and cl 6(1) applies Division 2 only to compensable in-, out- and same day patients. For the others the provider sets the fee under s 55, consistently with any policy framework. Asserted.
9. **A person treated as not eligible under HIA s 6(2) has no in-patient class.** Clause 12(2)(g)(i) excludes such a person from "ineligible in-patient", and every other in-patient class requires eligibility, compensation or veteran status. The encoding returns "the Order gives no in-patient class". Asserted.
10. **Divisions 1 and 2 each give way to the other.** Clause 5(2)(a) says Division 1 does not apply to a service charged under Division 2, and cl 6(2)(a) says the reverse. For a compensable out-patient's medicine, item 5 and item 8 both fit. The encoding takes item 8, the compensable patients' own item, at no more than $25. Not asserted (an observation; no compensable medicine case is in the case file).
11. **An out-patient medicine is not free for a Medicare-eligible public patient.** Division 1 item 5 charges "no more than $25" per item, or "no more than $7.70" for Gold or White Card veterans, pensioners and concessional beneficiaries. It is free only for holders of an entitlement card. Asserted.
12. **A parent staying with a sick child, or a breastfeeding mother, stays free; anyone else accompanying a patient pays $46 a day** (item 7). Asserted.
13. **The hospital can take its charge out of a patient's compensation even when it did not charge at the time.** Section 57A applies where the provider "did not charge the patient a compensable charge". Once compensation is "paid or payable", the charge is payable to the provider and recoverable from the patient who receives compensation. If the chief executive has given notice, the payer "must pay the compensable charge to the health service provider before paying the compensation to the patient" (s 57D). Payments from a scheme the recipient contributed to are not compensation (s 53B(1)(a)(ii), (iv)). Neither is a payment made before the 2023 amendments commenced (1 July 2023, from the compilation table). Asserted.
14. **The patient-facing duties are thin: a complaints procedure, and confidentiality with a $5,000 fine.** Section 34(2)(f) makes "an efficient and effective procedure for dealing with complaints" a function of every health service provider. Section 219(1) forbids collecting, using or disclosing information obtained under the Act, with a $5,000 fine. Section 220(1) lists the good-faith exceptions, including consent. Section 217 lets the chief executive disclose relevant health information without consent to anyone who, "in the opinion of the chief executive", has "a sufficient interest" in the patient's care. Asserted.
15. **Part 4 (cited by both events) is machinery.** It establishes health service providers and lists their functions and powers. Apart from s 34(2)(f), none of it is a rule the patient meets. Not asserted.
