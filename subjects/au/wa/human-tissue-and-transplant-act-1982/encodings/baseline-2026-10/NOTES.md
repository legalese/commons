## Seat

**The potential donor, living and after death, and the senior available next of kin.**

The Act is addressed to the designated officer of a hospital, to medical practitioners,
to the coroner and to the Minister. But the person it is applied to is the one whose
body it is, and, after death, the family member whose consent or objection counts. So
the decisions are phrased as those people would ask them:

- `Whose consent governs donation of my tissue after death`
- `May my tissue be removed after my death`
- `Does my relative's objection stop the removal`
- `Am I next of kin`, `Am I the senior available next of kin`
- `May my consent given during unconsciousness be acted on`
- `May I consent to giving this tissue while I am alive`
- `May my tissue be removed now under my consent`
- `May tissue be taken from my child`
- `Is my agreement void and an offence`, `What is the most I can be punished for trading in tissue`
- `May my identity as a donor be disclosed`

Where a provision addresses only an official, the encoding gives what it means for the
donor: what may be done to their body (ss 22, 23, 24), who may do it (s 24(1A)), and
whether their identity may be disclosed (s 34).

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone.** Followed. No regulation sets a number the encoded provisions use.
  The Commonwealth Australian Organ Donor Register is outside the Act. It is not read,
  and being on it is entered as a fact (see finding 1).
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the designated officer, the senior available
  next of kin, the coroner, medical practitioners, persons appointed under s 24A, a
  parent (s 11), the Minister.
- **Judgments handed to others are answered conditionally.** Followed. The designated
  officer's "is satisfied" and "has reason to believe" (ss 22(2), 23(1)), the coroner's
  consent, the Chief Health Officer's acceptance of qualifications (s 24(2)), the
  Minister's orders and approvals (ss 29D, 29E), and a practitioner's advice (ss 8, 9, 13,
  19) are all asked as facts.
- **The Act's own definitions are worked out from facts; borrowed terms are asked.**
  Followed. "Child", "next of kin" and "senior available next of kin" are computed.
  "Spouse", "de facto partner", "parent", "available" and "reportable death" are asked.
  Regenerative and nonregenerative tissue are asked as a kind of tissue.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** Followed: one deceased person with their
  family; one living donor's consent; one child's donation; one agreement.
- **The Act's own terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed.
- **Operative sections in, machinery out.** Followed. Designated officers and delegation
  (ss 4, 5, 5A), the Advisory Body (s 29F), codes of practice (ss 32A, 32B) and
  regulations (ss 35, 37) are out. Part 4 (postmortems), s 30 (advertising), Part 6 (stem
  cell lines) and s 33 (offences by those who remove tissue) are operative but outside
  the seat as the life events need it. They are left out and listed in the header.

- **jake k097** (2079-04-18). Jake dies at 72. He has been on the Register since 2047, and

  - `Am I next of kin` and `Am I the senior available next of kin`, for Ruby, Chloe and

  - `Have the section 24(2) declarations been made`;
  - `May this person remove my tissue`, for the kidneys.
- **daniel d092** (2078-10-21). Daniel dies at 82. He is on the Register and the family

  Priya, Anjali and Sofia, and Priya's objection.

## Why this scope

All four events cite s 22, so Part 3 is encoded whole: ss 22, 23, 24 and 24A(2), with
the s 3(1) definitions. Brain death (s 24(2)) is encoded because the seat asks about the
definition of death, and the Act's only answer is there. Living donation by adults
(ss 8, 9, 15, 16, 18, 20) and by children (ss 11-14, 17, 19) is encoded because two
events fall on an 18th birthday, and turning 18 is what changes. The ban on trading
(ss 29, 29A) is encoded as far as the donor meets it. s 34 is encoded because it is
what the Act promises the donor about their identity.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **The Act never mentions the Organ Donor Register.** In the deposit, "Register"
   appears only for the Therapeutic Goods Register (s 29B) and the Register of
   Undertakings (s 32B). Treating a registration as the deceased's having "expressed the
   wish for, or consented to, the removal after death" (s 22(2)(a)) is an **inference**.
   Whether that holds is still for the designated officer, "after making inquiries".
   Asserted, on that input.
2. **Donation is opt-in: there is no presumed consent.** s 22(2) has two limbs only:
   - (a) the deceased's own wish or consent;
   - (b) no reason to believe the deceased objected, and the senior available next of
     kin consents.

   With neither, there is no authority. This answers c026. Asserted.
3. **On the text, the family cannot override the deceased's own consent.** Paragraph (a)
   says nothing about next of kin, and s 22(5) on objections works through the
   senior-available-next-of-kin description, which belongs to paragraph (b). So Ruby's
   objection does not stop the removal from Jake (k097), and Priya's does not stop it
   from Daniel (d092). The family's "confirmation" in d092 has no role in the text.
   Hospital practice may differ, but that practice is not in the Act. Asserted.
4. **Who is senior in the lives.**
   - **Jake (k097).** Ruby, his adult daughter, is the senior available next of kin
     (s 3(1)(b)(ii)). Tahlia, his former wife, is in no subparagraph and is not next of
     kin at all. Chloe, his sister, is next of kin under (b)(iv), so she becomes senior
     only if Ruby is not available. His parents are not in the way: by the life's own
     facts Graham died in 2052 and Sue in 2060. This settles a caveat the first-pass
     encoding left open.
   - **Daniel (d092).** Priya, his wife, outranks Anjali and Luca, who outrank his sister
     Sofia.
   - **A deceased child.** A brother of 14 is not next of kin.

   Asserted.
5. **One objection among equals defeats the rest** (s 22(5)). With Priya unavailable,
   Anjali's consent is defeated by Luca's objection. An objection from a lower
   description has no effect: Ruby consents and Chloe objects, and the removal may go
   ahead. Two points are **readings, not rulings**: that s 22(5) reaches only the senior
   description, and that it does not ask whether the objector is "available". Asserted.
6. **The Act has no definition of death.** Its only rule on brain death is s 24(2). Where
   respiration and circulation are kept going artificially, no tissue may be removed
   unless 2 medical practitioners have each examined the person and declared
   "irreversible cessation of all function of the brain". Each must have at least 5
   years as a medical practitioner, and practice overseas counts (s 24(3)). One must be a
   specialist in general medicine, neurology or neurosurgery, or hold qualifications the
   Chief Health Officer accepts. A declaring practitioner may not then remove the tissue
   (s 24(1)), and neither may the designated officer. Jake's ventilation, and who
   declared, are **assumptions**: the life does not say. Asserted.
7. **Part 3 sets no minimum age for the deceased's own wish; 18 matters only for living
   donation.**
   - A recorded wish at 16 would satisfy s 22(2)(a) on the text.
   - At 18, an adult of sound mind may consent to give tissue (ss 8, 9). For tissue other
     than blood they must be advised and must sign in writing away from family and
     friends. Nonregenerative tissue may be given only for transplant to a living person.
   - Blood needs neither writing nor advice (s 18).
   - Nonregenerative tissue may be removed only after the 24 hours have expired, and not
     by the practitioner who gave the advice (s 16). Removal at exactly 24 hours is read
     as too early, but that boundary is not asserted.
   - So turning 18 in c026 and d021 switches on living donation, not the donor register.

   Asserted.
8. **A living child may never give nonregenerative tissue for transplant** (s 12(2)).
   - Regenerative tissue needs four things (s 13): a parent's written consent (a guardian
     is not a parent, s 11), medical advice to both, the child's understanding, and the
     child's agreement. And it may go only to a member of the child's family or a
     relative.
   - Either the parent or the child may revoke before the removal (s 14).
   - Blood needs a parent's consent, a practitioner's advice that it will not harm the
     child, and the child's agreement (s 19).

   Asserted.
9. **Selling tissue is void and an offence, before or after death.** An agreement to
   supply tissue from one's body "whether before or after" death for valuable
   consideration is void (s 29A(2)). Entering it carries up to 12 months' imprisonment or
   a $12 000 fine (s 29A(3)). Some agreements fall outside:
   - reimbursement of necessary expenses only is saved (s 29A(4)(a)), unless a Minister's
     order under s 29D(2) brings it back in;
   - a Minister's approval under s 29E takes an agreement outside;
   - so does the sale of embryos, sperm or eggs (s 29).

   Asserted.
10. **The donor's identity is protected** (s 34). It may not be made publicly known
    except under a court order or other legal requirement, for hospital administration or
    bona fide research, with the donor's consent, or where privileged. Asserted.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- **Read the Commonwealth side.** The Australian Organ and Tissue Donation and
  Transplantation Authority Act 2008 and the Register's rules would show whether a
  registration is a s 22(2)(a) "expressed wish" in law or only evidence of one.
- **Read WA Health's guidance and any case law on s 22.** Practice on family objection is
  the likely gap between this text and what happens.
- **Settle the readings.** Does s 22(5) reach only the senior description, and only
  available persons? Does "after the expiration of a period of 24 hours" exclude removal
  at exactly 24 hours?
- **Encode the rest.** Postmortems (Part 4), the s 29A(4)(b)-(f) exceptions and
  ss 29B-29E in detail, s 30 and s 33 are left out.
