# Coroners Act 1996 (WA) — baseline encoding

## Method

**Baseline.** Written straight from the deposited text with the `writing-l4-rules`
skill, from the seat of the individual to whom the law is applied, with the design
defaults ruled on 2026-10-18. No REFUSE. No pipeline, no coverage table, no
independent test pass, no human gate. Not for public use as advice.

One module, `cor-deaths.l4`, and one case file, `cor-cases-deaths.l4`. The case file
is generated by a script (scratchpad `gen_wa_coroners-act-1996_baseline.py`) from a
table of facts. The expected values were written by hand from the text. Every question
is exported with `@export` and `@desc` for Legalese Cloud.

## Edition

- **Act:** compilation from legislation.wa.gov.au (mrdoc 47642, retrieved
  7 September 2026), as deposited at `../../registers/source-bundle/mrdoc_47642.txt`.
  - Its compilation table runs to the Aboriginal Heritage Legislation Amendment and
    Repeal Act 2023 s 27 (in force 15 November 2023).
  - The State Superannuation (Transitional and Consequential Provisions) Act 2000
    s 36(2) is listed as uncommenced. It is not reflected.
- **Regulations:** none. The Coroners Regulations 1997 are not deposited, so three
  things are handled without them:
  - "prescribed circumstances" for a reportable death (s 3 limb (d)) are an input;
  - "prescribed circumstances" for an inquest (s 22(1)(f)) are an input;
  - any prescribed change to who is "senior next of kin" (s 37(5), "unless otherwise
    prescribed") is not applied.

## Seat

**The family of the deceased.** The Act is applied to them when someone in the family
dies: whether the death must be reported, whether they must report it, whether the
coroner takes the body, what they are told, what they may object to or ask for, and
when they get the body back. Most operative provisions address the coroner, the State
Coroner, doctors or carers. Each is encoded as what it means for the family.

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers.** Followed, so far as the
  deposit allows. The Act sets its own penalties and periods. The regulations, which
  would set prescribed circumstances, are not deposited, so those are inputs.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the coroner, the State Coroner, the Attorney
  General, the Supreme Court, the doctor present at or soon after the death, the person
  under whose care the deceased was held, a member of the Police Force, the pathologist,
  the next of kin and the senior next of kin.
- **Judgments handed to others answered conditionally.** Followed. These are inputs,
  marked "(coroner)" in the L4:
  - the coroner's determination of natural causes on a pathologist's opinion (s 19A);
  - the coroner's belief that a post mortem must be performed immediately (s 37(2));
  - the coroner's determination that touching the body is undesirable or dangerous
    (s 30(2));
  - the coroner's belief that access to evidence is not desirable or practicable
    (s 26A);
  - the directions of the Attorney General and the State Coroner (ss 22, 23).

  The "appears" limbs of "reportable death" are asked as facts as they appear.
- **The Act's own definitions worked out; borrowed terms asked.** Followed.
  - Worked out: "Western Australian death", "reportable death" (with ss 3A and 3B) and
    "senior next of kin" (s 37(5)).
  - Asked as facts: "person held in care", whose eleven categories are drawn from other
    Acts, and whether a doctor has certified under the Births, Deaths and Marriages
    Registration Act 1998 s 44.
- **One case is the natural unit.** One death.
- **The Act's own terms with a gloss.** Followed in the comments.
- **Operative sections in scope; machinery out.** Followed. Part 2 (the court and its
  officers), reports, regulation-making and review are out.

## The lives

- **chloe c086** (2052-08-19). Graham dies at 82 at home. The funeral director
  registers the death and a cremation permit is obtained. The scenario says of this Act
  only "s 17 (reportable deaths: not this one)".
  - The death is a Western Australian death but not a reportable one.
  - Neither Chloe nor Graham's GP must report it. Whom must Chloe report to? No one.
  - The coroner has no jurisdiction, so there is no investigation and no inquest.
  - The coroner does not hold the body, so the funeral director may take it.
  - Sue, who lived with Graham, is senior next of kin. Chloe is not, while Sue is
    available.
  - Variants test what would change the answer:
    - no s 44 certificate;
    - a sudden death;
    - a fall;
    - a death during an anaesthetic;
    - a death in a police lock-up;
    - voluntary assisted dying;
    - a death on holiday abroad.

The scenario does not say that the death was expected and natural, that his GP
certifies the cause under s 44, or that Sue lived with him. These are assumed in the
cases.

## Why this scope

The seat meets these parts of the Act:

- Part 3 (reporting);
- the coroner's jurisdiction and its exception (ss 19, 19A);
- the inquest provisions that decide whether there will be one, and the family's right
  to ask (ss 22-24);
- the next-of-kin rights scattered through Part 4 (ss 20, 25(4), 26A, 30(2), 35, 37);
- the release of the body (ss 29, 30).

What is left out is machinery or the coroner's own conduct of the case:

- Part 2;
- the findings and records (ss 25(1)-(3), 26-28);
- the powers of entry and the post mortem itself (ss 32-34);
- exhumation (s 38), the family's other objection right. It is named here as the most
  obvious next addition;
- the conduct of inquests (Part 5).

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **A death with no s 44 certificate is reportable however natural it is.** Limb (i)
   of "reportable death" catches any death in WA "where the cause of death has not been
   certified under section 44" of the Births, Deaths and Marriages Registration Act
   1998. Read literally, every death in WA is reportable from the moment of death until
   a doctor certifies, and the doctor has 48 hours to do so. The encoding asks whether a
   doctor "has certified, or will certify". That is an inference. Asserted.
2. **The two Acts are circular on the doctor's certificate.** BDM s 44(5) excuses a
   doctor from certifying where s 17(3) requires the doctor to report the death. Under
   limb (i), a death the doctor does not certify becomes reportable. So a doctor who
   declines to certify is then required to report, and is thereby excused from
   certifying. The text gives no way out of the loop. Not asserted. Recorded for a
   lawyer.
3. **The cremation question, from this side.** The Cremation Act baseline found that,
   read literally, a reportable death with no s 44 certificate cannot be cremated.
   Cremation Act s 8A(a) has no exception for a coroner's case. This Act does not help:
   - The coroner's s 29 certificate permits "burial, cremation or other disposal of the
     body". That releases the body from the coroner's control (s 30(1)).
   - Nothing in this Act amends or displaces Cremation Act s 8A(a).
   - With limb (i), the deaths that lack a s 44 certificate are exactly the deaths that
     are reportable for that reason.

   On the literal reading, then, the coroner may release a body "for cremation" which
   the medical referee may not permit to be cremated. The Cremation Regulations r 12,
   which accept a coroner's certificate, suggest practice runs otherwise. Asserted (the
   body is released on the s 29 certificate).
4. **Unexpected deaths rarely escape investigation.** Section 19A lets the coroner stop
   where the death is natural and reportable "solely because it appears to have been
   unexpected". An unexpected death that no doctor will certify is also reportable under
   limb (i), so it is not reportable "solely" because unexpected. On the text, s 19A
   works only where a doctor certifies anyway. Asserted.
5. **Anyone aware must report, to a coroner or the police; doctors and carers must
   report to a coroner.** The general duty is "immediately after he or she becomes
   aware" (s 17(1)). A doctor present at or soon after the death (s 17(3)) and the
   person under whose care the deceased was held (s 17(5)) must report to a coroner. A
   family member who has reasonable grounds to believe the death has already been
   reported need not report. Each duty carries a penalty of up to $1 000. Asserted.
6. **The second doctor is relieved of reporting, not of helping.** Section 17(4) excuses
   the other doctors present from s 17(3) once one has reported, but they "must give to
   the coroner ... any information which may help". The encoding treats knowing of the
   first report as reasonable grounds under s 17(1) as well. That is an inference.
   Asserted.
7. **The family has no right to an inquest, only to ask for one, and then to go to the
   Supreme Court.**
   - An inquest is compulsory only for a death in care, a death caused by police action
     or caused while in care, a death in prescribed circumstances, or on a direction
     (s 22(1), s 23(2)).
   - Otherwise anyone may ask in writing with reasons (s 24(1a)). The coroner may refuse
     with written reasons.
   - The applicant then has 7 days from notice of the refusal to apply to the Supreme
     Court.
   - If there is no reply within 3 months, they may apply then. The text sets no closing
     date for that route.

   Asserted.
8. **Senior next of kin is a strict order, with a gap for ties.**
   - Section 37(5) ranks a spouse or adult de facto partner who lived with the deceased
     first, then a spouse, an adult child, a parent, an adult sibling, the executor and
     the emergency contact.
   - An executor who is also an adult child takes the child's higher place.
   - Two adult children who are both available share the first place. The Act does not
     say which of them is senior. The encoding returns that answer as such.

   Asserted.
9. **Every next of kin, not only the senior, is told, may view and may touch.**
   Sections 20, 25(4) and 30(2) speak of "any of the deceased person's next of kin
   under section 37(5)". These are read as meaning anyone on the s 37(5) list. That is
   an inference. Only the senior next of kin may object to a post mortem (s 37), have
   their own doctor present (s 35) or ask for access to the evidence (s 26A). Asserted.
10. **An objection to a post mortem buys 2 clear working days, not until the court
    decides.**
    - Once the senior next of kin is notified that the coroner thinks a post mortem
      necessary, it must wait 2 clear working days, or until any Supreme Court
      extension ends (s 37(2)).
    - The coroner may proceed at once if the coroner believes it must be performed
      immediately.
    - Nothing in s 37 holds the post mortem back merely because an application to the
      Supreme Court is pending.

    Asserted.
11. **The body is released only on the coroner's certificate, and a pending s 36 request
    holds it back.**
    - For a reportable death with the body in WA, the coroner controls the body until
      the s 29 certificate is issued (s 30(1)).
    - The certificate must issue "as soon as reasonably possible".
    - It must not issue while anyone's request for a post mortem is refused and the
      2 clear working days to go to the Supreme Court are running, or while such an
      application is pending (s 29(2)).
    - After a Supreme Court order for a post mortem, only the State Coroner may issue it
      (s 29(3)).

    Asserted.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- The Coroners Regulations 1997 should be deposited and read. They set prescribed
  circumstances, may vary "senior next of kin", and contain Form 4, which the Cremation
  Regulations accept.
- A lawyer's view on findings 1-3 (limb (i), the circle with BDM s 44(5), and
  cremation after a coroner's case). This should be taken together with the Cremation
  Act baseline's finding 5.
- Exhumation (s 38) and the inquest rights of an interested person (s 44) would
  complete the family's rights.
- "Person held in care" could be worked out from its eleven categories rather than
  asked.
- No case law was searched. There has been no independent test pass and no human gate.
