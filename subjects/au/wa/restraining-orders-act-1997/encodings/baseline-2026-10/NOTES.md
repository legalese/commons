## Seat

**The person threatened: the person seeking to be protected.** In the lives, that is Jake at 29, for his household.
The Act speaks to the court, to police officers and to the person bound. Each provision is encoded as what it means for the person who wants protection:

- which order can protect them;
- who may apply;
- whether they can get protection before the respondent is heard;
- whether the police can act on the spot;
- how long the protection lasts;
- what happens to the respondent who breaches it.

The questions are phrased that way: "Which restraining order can protect me", "May this person apply for me", "Can the police make an order to protect me now", "How many months would the final order protect me", "What is the most the respondent can be fined for a breach".

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers.** Followed. No instrument sets a number used here.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the person seeking to be protected, the respondent, the applicant (the person themselves, a police officer, a parent or guardian, a child welfare officer, a guardian under the Guardianship and Administration Act 1990), the court, the authorised magistrate, and the police officer.
- **A judgment handed to someone else is answered conditionally.** Followed. These are asked as facts:
  - whether the respondent "is likely again" to commit the violence (ss 10D(1)(a), 11A(a));
  - whether the person has "reasonable grounds to apprehend" it (ss 10D(1)(b), 11A(b));
  - whether "special circumstances" make an FVRO inappropriate (s 10D(2));
  - whether an order is "appropriate in the circumstances" (ss 11A, 34(b));
  - whether, "unless restrained", the respondent is likely to intimidate or offend, damage property, or breach the peace (s 34(a));
  - whether the magistrate is satisfied a matter is urgent or impractical to bring in person (s 20(1));
  - the police officer's beliefs under s 30A;
  - the s 61A(6) exception to mandatory imprisonment.
- **The Act's own definitions worked out, borrowed terms asked.** Followed. Worked out: "family relationship" (s 4, from its seven limbs), "family violence" (s 5A(1)), "personal violence" (s 6(2)), "imagined personal relationship" (s 3). Asked as facts: "related" (s 4(2), which turns on cultural, social or religious background), and "assault" and "stalking", which the Act borrows from The Criminal Code.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** Followed: one person seeking to be protected and one respondent (`My case`).
- **The Act's own terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed (FVRO, VRO, MRO, police order, interim order, final order).
- **Operative sections in, machinery out.** Followed. Out: s 17 (authorised magistrates), s 30I (review), s 73 (regulations), the behaviour change programme approvals in Part 1C, and the forms.

## Why this scope

The plan entry names the FVRO and VRO Parts and Part 3 (misconduct restraining orders).
The brief adds who may apply, the grounds, interim orders, police orders, duration and breach.
The scope is what a threatened person meets on the way to an order and after it: the definitions that sort the three orders (ss 4, 5A, 6); the grounds for each (ss 10D, 11A, 12A, 34, 35A); the bars on respondents (ss 7, 50); who may apply (ss 24A, 25, 38); the fast routes (telephone orders, ss 18, 20; first hearings in the respondent's absence, ss 26, 29; police orders, ss 30A, 30F); how long orders last (ss 16, 16A, 16B, 31, 37, 50A); and breach (ss 61, 61A, 61B(3)).

Left out, because they shape the terms of an order or the procedure after it rather than whether the person is protected:

- the matters the court weighs (ss 10F, 12, 35), which come in only through the court's final satisfaction;
- the restraints themselves (ss 10G, 13, 30C, 36), listed in comments only;
- firearms and explosives orders (ss 14, 14A);
- FVROs for a child exposed to family violence (s 10E);
- behaviour management orders (Part 1C);
- conduct agreements and consent orders (ss 10H, 41);
- the respondent's objection procedure beyond the 21 days (ss 32, 33);
- respondents in prison (s 16A(3));
- orders made in other proceedings or on conviction (ss 63, 63A);
- variation and cancellation (Part 5);
- evidence and special conferences (Parts 4, 5A);
- service (Part 6), beyond the police order rules;
- the s 62 defences;
- police entry and search (ss 62A-62G);
- interstate and foreign orders (Parts 7, 7A);
- the MRO ground in s 34(a)(iv) (The Criminal Code s 70A(2A), animal source food production).

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **The relationship picks the order before the conduct does.** Within a family relationship (s 4) only an FVRO is open: ss 12A and 35A forbid a VRO or an MRO between people in a family relationship. Outside one, only a VRO or an MRO is open, because family violence (s 5A) is defined as violence towards "a family member". Asserted.
2. **"Family relationship" is wide.** It includes former spouses and de facto partners, relatives (read with cultural background and in-laws), a child who stays regularly with the other person, past intimate or "other personal" relationships, and a partner's former partner. Asserted for former de facto partners and for strangers.
3. **An MRO needs no past conduct.** s 34 asks only whether, "unless restrained", the respondent is likely to intimidate or offend the person, damage their property, or breach the peace, and whether an MRO is appropriate. FVROs and VROs can also rest on a reasonable apprehension alone (ss 10D(1)(b), 11A(b)). Asserted.
4. **Paint and glue are an MRO case, not a VRO case, unless violence is threatened.** Personal violence (s 6(2)) means assault, kidnapping, stalking, or a threat of assault or kidnapping. A threat of detriment over a debt is none of these. That fits the Criminal Code baseline's reading of the paint (s 338A, a threat of detriment) and the scenario's own answer, "a misconduct restraining order for the household". If the paint threatened to hurt someone, it would be a threat under s 6(2)(d) and a VRO would be open. Asserted.
5. **An FVRO is mandatory once the grounds are made out.** s 10D(2): "the court must make the order unless there are special circumstances". A VRO or MRO is discretionary ("appropriate in the circumstances"). Asserted.
6. **Only an individual can be bound.** s 7: the person bound "must be natural persons". If the lender is a company, the order must name the individual who did it. No order may be made against a child under 10 (s 50). Asserted.
7. **A person under 16 cannot apply for an FVRO themselves, but can for a VRO or MRO.** s 24A(1)(a) sets 16; ss 25 and 38 set no age. A police officer may apply for anyone, and a parent or guardian for a child. Ruby (3) can be protected by an application her parent makes. Asserted.
8. **There is no fast route to an MRO.** Telephone applications (s 3, s 18) and first hearings in the respondent's absence (s 26) are for FVROs and VROs only. For an MRO the registrar must summons the respondent (s 39). The order is made only at a hearing, though it can be made in the respondent's absence if they were served and do not come (s 40(2), not encoded). Asserted.
9. **Police orders are for family violence only, and last at most 72 hours.** s 30A turns on family violence, so a police officer cannot make one against the lender. A police order lapses if it is not served within 24 hours (s 30F(1)(b)) and cannot be renewed (s 30H). As read, the "necessary to ensure the safety of a person" words govern only s 30A(1)(b), the apprehension limb; that reading is an inference from where the words sit. Asserted.
10. **Default durations differ.** An FVRO or VRO made at a final order hearing lasts 2 years if no period is specified (ss 16A, 16B); an MRO lasts 1 year (s 37(2)). A final order against a child is capped at 6 months (s 50A). An interim order becomes final if the respondent does not object within 21 days (ss 31, 32(2)), and a telephone order lasts 3 months at most (s 16(4)(d)). Asserted.
11. **Breaching an MRO is a fine-only offence.** s 61(2): a fine of $1 000. An FVRO, VRO or police order breach carries $10 000, 2 years' imprisonment, or both (s 61(1), (1A), (2a)). Repeated breach (s 61A) does not count MRO breaches, so for Jake's household it never applies. For an FVRO, VRO or police order, a third breach conviction within 2 years (offences on one day counting once) must bring imprisonment, unless the s 61A(6) exception is found. Asserted.
12. **The 2-year limit for prosecuting a breach does not cover MROs.** s 61(6) names only subsections (1), (1A) and (2a). The limit for an MRO breach comes from outside this Act; it is not encoded. Not asserted.
13. **The protected person does not offend by aiding a breach.** s 61B(3). Asserted.
14. **The grounds and the procedure sit in different Parts.** The FVRO grounds are in Part 1B and the VRO grounds in Part 2. Part 2A, which the scenario cites, holds the shared procedure: duration, telephone and in-person applications, and police orders. Not asserted.

## Q&A layer (seat and situations as parameters)

Added 6 November 2026, after the model of the Graffiti Vandalism Act 2016 encoding's seat layer (its design decisions D2, D4 and D8). The baseline's files are unchanged: the layer is added, nothing is replaced. Two new files:

- `ro-qa.l4` imports `prelude` and `ro-orders`. It has 33 exported questions.
- `ro-qa-cases.l4` imports the same and `ro-qa`. It has 171 cases.

### Roles

`DECLARE Role IS ONE OF`:

- `I want to be protected from someone`: the baseline's seat, the person seeking to be protected (22 questions, one for each baseline export).
- `someone wants a restraining order against me, or one has been made against me`: the respondent, the person bound (11 questions).

Why: the Act gives the right to apply to the person seeking to be protected, and puts its duties, its time limits and its offences on the person bound. The court, the registrar, the authorised magistrate and the police are never seats. A parent, guardian or child welfare officer applying for a child asks the protected person's questions for the child; "May this person apply for the order to protect me" already covers them, so they are not a separate seat. Every exported question takes `my role` IS A Role first, with `@desc Which of these describes you`, and for the other role answers `does not arise for you` without reading any other input.

### The situation input

One input, shared by both roles with the same name, type and `@desc`, always second: `the order so far`, of type `The order so far` (`no order has been made yet`, `an interim order has been made`, `a final order has been made`, `a police order has been made`). The Act ties each of these questions to an order having been made:

| Questions | Arise when the order so far is | Does not arise for |
|---|---|---|
| Protected: `How many days does the respondent have to object to my interim order`. Respondent: `Within how many days must I return the endorsement copy of the interim order`, `If I do not return the endorsement copy does the interim order become final` (ss 31, 32) | an interim order | no order yet, a final order, a police order |
| Protected: `How many months will the final order protect me`. Respondent: `How many months will the final order bind me` (ss 16A, 16B, 37, 50A) | an interim order (which becomes final, s 32) or a final order | no order yet, a police order |
| Respondent: `For how many hours does the police order bind me`, `Within how many hours must the police order be served on me`, `Can the police extend or renew the police order against me` (ss 30F, 30H) | a police order | no order yet, an interim order, a final order |
| Protected: `What is the most the respondent can be fined for breaching my order`, `What is the longest the respondent can be imprisoned for breaching my order`, `Must the respondent be imprisoned for breaching my order again`, `Do I commit an offence if I help the respondent breach my order`. Respondent: `What is the most I can be fined for breaching the order`, `What is the longest I can be imprisoned for breaching the order`, `Must I be imprisoned if I am convicted of breaching the order again`, `Within how long must a prosecution for my breach be started` (ss 61, 61A, 61B) | any order | no order yet |

16 questions take it (6 of the protected person's, 10 of the respondent's). The protected person's other 16 questions are about getting an order (the relationship, the violence, which order, who may apply, telephone orders, interim orders, police orders on the spot, the fixed durations of telephone and police orders) and arise in every situation, so they take none; so does the respondent's `Can a restraining order be made against me at all`.

### Decisions

- **Q1. A police order is not asked its kind.** Where the order so far is a police order, the breach questions pass `a police order` to the baseline's rules and do not read `the order made` (helper `the kind bound by`). Where it is an interim or final order, the kind given is used as it is; an interim MRO cannot be made (baseline finding 8), but the encoding does not reject one.
- **Q2. The protected person's breach and duration questions now arise only once an order is made.** In the baseline they could be asked before applying ("what would happen if they breach it"). The Act ties breach to "a person who is bound by" an order (s 61) and the duration to the order made (`months specified in the order`), so, as with the graffiti notice (D8.10), the order is the situation. Someone deciding whether to apply who answers "no order yet" is not told the penalties. Michael may prefer to leave these four ungated.
- **Q3. Two fixed durations stay ungated for the protected person.** The 72 hours and the 24 hours of a police order, and the 3 months of a telephone order, describe the routes the person is choosing between, so they arise in every situation. The respondent's police order questions take the situation, since they are asked of an order made against them.
- **Q4. Answers for the protected person are the baseline's own, wrapped.** Booleans become `Yes or no` through `yes if`; numbers become `Amount`, `Months`, `Days` or `Hours`; `Which restraining order can protect me` is wrapped in `Which order, if it arises`. Each question has a case that its answer equals the baseline export's on the same facts (22 cases).
- **Q5. Inputs whose `@desc` differs by seat have different names.** The baseline names its kind-of-order input `the order` in every question, with four `@desc` texts. The layer uses `the order I want` ("The order you want") for applying and `the order made` (one `@desc`) for an order made, in both roles. The count of earlier breach days is `days the respondent committed breaches they were convicted of, in the 2 years before` for the protected person and `days I committed breaches I was convicted of, in the 2 years before` for the respondent. `my age in years` has one `@desc` ("Your age in years") in both roles. The baseline's record inputs (`r`, `d`, `c`) are renamed `how we are connected`, `what the respondent has done`, `my case`.
- **Q6. Two respondent rules are helpers in the layer,** since the baseline does not export them: s 32(2) (an interim order not answered becomes final at the end of the 21 days) and s 61(6) (2 years to prosecute an FVRO, VRO or police order breach; none set for an MRO, baseline finding 12). `Can a restraining order be made against me at all` uses the baseline's own number for s 50 (`the youngest respondent`), with s 7 met by the person asking.
- **Q7. "Not read" for the baseline's enumerations and records.** `the order so far` is read only through helpers that list every value with no OTHERWISE (graffiti finding 31). The baseline reads `Kind of order` through CONSIDER ... OTHERWISE, and its records only through fields; the cases still pass ASSUMEd values for them, and the parameter script and the code show the role and situation are checked first (graffiti D8.8).

### Not covered for the respondent

- Applying to set aside a final order made because the endorsement copy was not returned (s 32(5)-(10)), the objection hearing (s 33) and variation or cancellation (Part 5).
- The s 62 defences to a breach, and s 61B(2), (2A) (when the protected person's aiding is not a mitigating factor).
- The terms an order may contain (ss 10G, 13, 36, 30C), firearms (ss 14, 14A), and orders made on conviction (ss 63, 63A).
- Where proceedings for a breach are brought (s 61(3): the Children's Court for a child, otherwise the Magistrates Court).
