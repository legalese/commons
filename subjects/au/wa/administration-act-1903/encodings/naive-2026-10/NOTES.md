## Why this Act, and why scoped

This encoding answers requirement **au/wa:REQ-0002** in `subjects/au/wa/requirements.jsonl`
(priority 1). The three Western Australian cradle-to-grave lives raise it 10 times:

- chloe c087 (Graham's will, 2052), c088 (Sue dies intestate, 2060), c096 (Liam dies, 2083), c098 (Chloe dies, 2088)
- jake k084 (his father's will), k088 (his mother's intestacy), k098 (his own intestacy, 2079)
- daniel d074 (his father's will, 2052), d086 (his mother's will, 2069), d093 (his own estate, 2078)

The questions they put to this Act:

- Which grant does a death with a will and an executor call for?
- Who takes when Sue dies intestate leaving two children? (c088 and k088 expect "half each")
- Who takes when Jake dies divorced and intestate leaving one child, Ruby? Must there be
  a grant, and may the Public Trustee act? (k098)
- Does Jake take anything under a will that leaves everything to his mother? (k084)

Scope:

- **Encoded.** The s 14 Table in full (the item turns on who survives), with s 14(2b),
  (3) and (4); s 15 (de facto partners); s 12A (children of unmarried parents);
  s 13(1a) (land held as tenants in common); ss 6, 7, 25, 33 and 36 (which grant, and
  who may be granted administration); s 55 (estates under $10 000); s 139 (bank
  deposits paid without a grant); and s 71 (when a will may be put in evidence).
- **Not encoded.** The Fourth Schedule (the surviving spouse's election to take the
  home), insolvent estates, sureties, special and limited grants, accounts, resealing of
  foreign grants (Part III), caveats (Part IV), and infants' shares.
- **Left to other Acts.** Family Provision Act claims (which s 60A preserves),
  survivorship between joint tenants, the validity of a will, superannuation, and the
  Public Trustee's own powers.

## What the Act turns out to say

### 1. Section 25 is not about probate, and Part IV is caveats

Seven of the ten events cite "s 25" for a death **with** a will: c087 ("s 25 (grant of
probate), Part IV"), c096, c098, k084, d074, d086 and d093. But s 25 is "Persons
entitled to administration", and it governs only "the estate of a person dying
intestate".

Probate comes from s 6 ("grant probate of the will") and s 7 ("probate to one or more of
the executors named in any will"). Where there is no fit executor, s 33 (an infant sole
executor) or s 36 (no executor, or one not "willing and competent" or "resident out of
Western Australia") gives administration with the will annexed instead.

Part IV, cited in c087, is "Caveats" (ss 63-64). Graham's, Chloe's and Oliver's wills
point to **probate**; Sue's and Jake's intestacies point to **letters of
administration**. Asserted.

### 2. Sue's estate: half each, as the lives expect, and the house as tenants in common

Sue is widowed in 2052 and leaves two children, so item 5 applies: "issue but no husband
or wife". Under s 14(2b)(b) the property is "divided into as many equal parts as there
are children" who survived or left surviving issue. Chloe and Jake take half each, which
matches c088 and k088.

The house came to Sue by survivorship (c087), so it is in her estate. Under s 13(1a)
land held for two or more people "shall be held as if it had been devised to those
persons as tenants in common". This answers Chloe's question 12, "Mum had no will. Who
gets the house?". Asserted.

### 3. Jake's estate: Ruby takes all and may administer it; the Act alone gives the Public Trustee no route while she is available

Jake divorced in 2039, and no later partner is recorded, so item 5 and s 14(2b)(a) apply:
"the only entitled issue ... is entitled to all". At 46, and "entitled in distribution",
Ruby may be granted administration (s 25(1)(a)).

Under this Act, "any other person" (the Public Trustee included, s 3) may be granted
administration only if no entitled person is "resident within the jurisdiction and fit
to be so entrusted", or if that person "fails, when duly cited, to appear" (s 25(1)(b)).
The scenario says the Public Trustee administers the estate, which must therefore rest on
the Public Trustee Act 1941. That Act is not encoded here.

The small-estate route in s 55 (apply direct to the Principal Registrar) is for property
"not exceeding $10 000". The $38,000 alone is over that limit. Asserted.

### 4. The bank may pay without a grant: $1,200 in the text, $50,000 in the notes

Under s 139, an ADI may pay a deposit to a widow, widower, parent, child or de facto
partner without any grant. The deposit must not exceed "$1 200, or such other amount as
may for the time being be declared by proclamation". Two further conditions apply: one
month must have passed, and in that month no grant was produced and no notice of a will
or of an application was given. The deposit's note 4 says the amount "is now declared to
be $50 000". The ceiling is therefore an input.

Whether Jake's $38,000 is a bank deposit is the encoder's assumption. With the
$50 000 ceiling, the bank may pay it to Ruby. The apartment is real estate, and s 8 vests
real estate in an executor or administrator only "upon the grant", so the apartment still
needs one (an inference from s 8, not encoded). Asserted for the deposit.

### 5. No death duty in this Act

Part V is still headed "Duties on deceased persons' estates and succession duties".
Every charging section in it (ss 65-70A, 72-137) is marked "Deleted: No. 80 of 1973
s. 3". Only s 71 remains, and it says no will is admissible in evidence before a grant,
except in criminal proceedings, on an application for a grant, or in proceedings on its
validity. For c098 and d093 ("any duty?"), this Act imposes none. That finding is not
asserted, since there is nothing to encode. The s 71 rule is asserted.

### 6. A will that disposes of everything leaves nothing for the Table (k084)

s 14(1) distributes only "the property as to which they die intestate". Graham's will
leaves everything to Sue, so Jake takes nothing under this Act, which matches k084's "no".
Whether he could claim under the Family Provision Act 1972 is outside this encoding
(s 60A preserves that Act's s 6). Asserted.

### 7. The Table's sums can move by Minister's order

The Act states $472 000 (item 2), $705 000 (item 3) and $56 500 (items 3(b)(i) and 6) as
"original sums" (s 14A(1)). The Minister may declare others, indexed to average weekly
earnings and rounded up to $500 (s 14A(3)), and must review them every second year
(s 14B). The sum that applies is the one in force at the death (s 14(1B)). The 2023 and
2025 Orders are named in the deposit but their figures are not, so every function takes
the sums as an input.

With the original sums, a spouse and two children sharing $1,072,000 net get $672,000
for the spouse and $200,000 for each child. With one child, s 14(3) changes the residue
split to halves: $772,000 for the spouse and $300,000 for the child. The interest on the
spouse's sum is 5% a year (s 14(4)). The deposit prints "$$472 000" in s 14(4)(a), a
typographical slip. Asserted, including one invented declared sum.

### 8. De facto partners: two years to take, five to displace a spouse (s 15)

A de facto partner of at least two years, where there is no spouse, takes what a spouse
would take.

Where there is a spouse with whom the intestate did not live during that period:

- after two years, the de facto partner and the spouse split the spouse's entitlement;
- after five years, the de facto partner takes it all.

A partner of one year takes nothing, and the children take under item 5. Asserted.

### 9. Children of unmarried parents count (s 12A)

The relationship is "determined irrespective of whether the parents are or have been
married". Where they never married, s 12A(2)(b) recognises it only if parentage was
admitted or established in the parent's lifetime. Where the claim benefits the parent,
it must also have been admitted or established in the child's lifetime.

Ruby was born before her parents married, and they married later that year, so she
counts either way. Asserted.

  provision belong in their own encodings. The lives' answers on the house and the super
  come from there, not from this Act.
- s 14(4) interest is simple interest on whole years only. Part payments and
  appropriation dates are not modelled.
- No case law or Supreme Court probate practice was consulted.
