# Civil Liability Act 2002 (WA) — baseline encoding

## Method

**Method: baseline.** Written straight from the deposited text with the
`writing-l4-rules` skill, from the seat of the individual to whom the law is
applied, with the design defaults ruled on 2026-10-18. No `REFUSE`. Checked with
the stable `l4` of VS Code extension 1.6.98. No pipeline, no coverage table, no
independent test pass, no human gate. Not for public use as advice.

There was no earlier encoding of this Act.

Every question is published with `@export` and a plain-words description, and
every input carries an `@desc`, so the module can be deployed to Legalese Cloud as
it stands.

## Edition

- **The Act:** the Civil Liability Act 2002 (No. 35 of 2002), compilation as
  deposited at `../../registers/source-bundle/mrdoc_49335.txt` (legislation.wa.gov.au
  mrdoc 49335, retrieved 7 September 2026). Its s 3A Table carries the amendments
  by the Workers Compensation and Injury Management Act 2023 (No. 21 of 2023) and
  by No. 32 of 2024 (dust diseases).
- **No regulations or notices.** The plan names the indexed thresholds "published
  in the Government Gazette". The Act fixes how they are reached (s 4) and the
  2002-03 starting figures (ss 10(1), 13(1)). The Minister's yearly notice is "for
  public information only" (ss 10(4), 13(4)) and none is deposited. So the amounts
  for any later year are inputs.
- **The s 9(3) formula** was an image the `.txt` lost. It is transcribed by hand
  from the authorised Word rendering of the same document (mrdoc 49335, fetched
  2026-10-21) in `../../registers/source-bundle/mrdoc_49335.formulas.md`, and
  encoded from there.

## Seat

**The injured person claiming damages.** That person asks:

- "Does this Act cover my claim, or does another Act?"
- "What thresholds and caps limit my damages?"
- "Will I be held partly to blame?"
- "Does an obvious risk, a risk warning or a waiver defeat my claim?"

The Act speaks almost entirely to courts: what a court "cannot award", when a
defendant "is not liable". Each rule is encoded as what it means for the injured
person.

Each 2026-10-18 default:

- **The Act alone, plus the instruments that set its numbers.** Followed. No
  instrument sets the numbers. The Wage Price Index and average weekly earnings
  are the Australian Statistician's, so they are inputs, as are the year's Amounts
  A, B and C.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the plaintiff (the injured person), the
  defendant, the court, a parent or other adult (for risk warnings to a child), the
  occupier of the place, the Minister (only as publisher of the amounts).
- **Judgments handed to others are asked as inputs.** Followed. The inputs are:
  - the court's assessment of non-pecuniary loss (s 9) and of the value of care
    (s 12);
  - whether a risk was foreseeable, not insignificant, and one a reasonable person
    would have guarded against (ss 5B, 5K);
  - whether a risk was "significant" or "obvious" (ss 5E, 5F);
  - whether the court is satisfied intoxication was not self-induced (s 5L(2)).
- **The Act's own definitions worked out from facts.** Followed for:
  - "personal injury damages";
  - "recreational activity";
  - "child" and "incompetent person" (s 5I(16));
  - the start dates of Parts 1A and 2, with the rule for an unknown date.

  The borrowed terms are asked as facts: whether the Motor Vehicle (Third Party
  Insurance) Act 1943 applies to the damages, and whether Part 7 Division 2 of the
  Workers Compensation and Injury Management Act 2023 does.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** One claim by one injured person.
- **The Act's terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed in comments.
- **Operative sections in, machinery out.** Followed. Left out:
  - Part 3 (advertising and touting), which addresses lawyers and touts, not the
    injured person;
  - s 22 (regulations);
  - ss 2, 5 (commencement, Crown).

## The lives

- **Jake, k044 (12 April 2029, aged 22): a delivery rider and contractor on an
  e-bike; a car door opens and fractures his wrist; no workers' compensation; a CTP
  claim against the driver's insurer.** The questions:
  - `Which limits apply to my damages`: if the door was part of "the driving", so
    that the Motor Vehicle (Third Party Insurance) Act applies, that Act's limits
    apply and not Part 2. If not, Part 2 applies to his negligence claim against
    whoever opened the door;
  - `Which of Part 1A applies to my claim`: all of it, either way;
  - `How much general damages can I be awarded`: an illustrative $20,000
    assessment gives $8,000 under Part 2, using the 2002-03 amounts;
  - `How much lost earnings can count`: about $600 a week, far below the cap;
  - `Will I be held contributorily negligent`: not if riding sober and with care;
    presumed if drunk, even on a CTP claim;
  - `Does the dangerous recreational activity rule defeat my claim`: no, because
    delivery riding is work, not recreation.

  Whether Jake is a worker is the workers' compensation baseline's question
  (there: no). The contrast case, a worker suing his employer, is asserted here:
  then neither Part 1A nor Part 2 applies.

## Why this scope

The life cites Part 2's thresholds. But s 3A decides whether Part 2 reaches Jake's
claim at all, so s 3A is in, as are the dates in ss 5A and 6. The seat also meets
the parts of Part 1A that defeat or cut a claim. These are:

- the general duty (s 5B);
- contributory negligence and intoxication (ss 5K, 5L);
- obvious and inherent risks (ss 5N-5P);
- recreational activities (ss 5E-5J), which the brief names.

Left out, each for a reason:

- Part 1A Division 7 (professional negligence) and Part 1B (mental harm): narrower
  claims than the life needs;
- Part 1C (public bodies), Part 1CA (volunteer staff) and Part 1D (good
  samaritans): protections for defendants of particular kinds;
- Part 1E (apologies);
- Part 1F (proportionate liability): it excludes personal injury;
- s 13A (CISS): the Act withdraws it from CTP claims anyway (finding 3);
- Part 2 Divisions 4 and 5 (structured settlements, dust diseases), Part 2A (child
  sexual abuse) and Part 3 (advertising and touting).

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **A CTP claim escapes Part 2 entirely.** The Jake scenario says his CTP claim
   runs "under the Civil Liability Act's thresholds". The s 3A Table says
   otherwise. For "Damages to which the Motor Vehicle (Third Party Insurance) Act
   1943 applies", Parts 1C, 1E and 2 "(other than section 10A and Division 4)" do
   not apply (item 2). So the general damages threshold, the earnings cap and the
   home care limits in this Act do not touch a CTP claim. The Motor Vehicle (Third
   Party Insurance) Act's own ss 3C-3F do, as that Act's baseline encodes. Part 2
   reaches Jake only if the door was not "the driving" and he sues the person who
   opened it in plain negligence. Asserted.
2. **Part 1A still reaches a CTP claim.** Item 2 does not list Part 1A. So
   contributory negligence, the intoxication presumption and the obvious-risk rules
   apply to a CTP claim. Asserted.
3. **s 13A is withdrawn from the claims it was written for.** s 13A bars damages
   for care needs of a "motor vehicle injury or workplace injury" while the person
   is in the CISS. But it sits in Part 2 Division 3, which item 2 disapplies from
   CTP damages and item 3 from employer claims under Part 7 Division 2. The
   encoder's reading: it bites only on claims outside both, such as a workplace
   injury claimed against a third party. Not asserted.
4. **A worker suing the employer leaves the whole Act behind.** Item 3 takes away
   Parts 1A, 1B, 1C, 1D, 1E and 2, keeping only s 10A and Divisions 4 and 5. The
   workers' compensation Act's own threshold governs. Asserted.
5. **Intentional harm, tobacco and dust keep only s 10A.** An assault or sexual
   offence loses Part 1A and Part 2's limits altogether (item 1). Tobacco, asbestos
   and silica keep only ss 5A-5D of Part 1A (items 4, 6). Aviation claims lose
   everything (item 5). Asserted.
6. **The general damages threshold.** At or below Amount A, nothing. Between A and
   C, only the excess over A (s 9(1), (2)). Asserted.
7. **The middle band closes the gap to full damages.** Between C and A + C,
   damages are capped at the excess of the assessment over "Amount A − (Amount
   assessed − Amount C)" (s 9(3); the formula, an image lost from the `.txt`, is
   in `mrdoc_49335.formulas.md`). With A = $12,000 and C = $36,500, an assessment of
   $40,000 gives at most $31,500 and $44,000 gives $39,500; the cap reaches the
   whole assessment at A + C. Asserted.
8. **No ceiling on general damages.** At A + C or more, s 9 makes no deduction, and
   unlike the CTP Act (its Amount A) this Act sets no maximum. Asserted.
9. **Indexing rounds to $500, ties up.** Each year's amount is last year's varied
   by the WA Wage Price Index and rounded to the nearest $500, "$250 more than a
   multiple of $500 being rounded off to the next highest" (s 4(3)). The Gazette
   notice does not change the amount even if it is wrong (s 10(4)). Asserted.
10. **Earnings above 3 times average weekly earnings are disregarded** (s 11), on
    the full-time adult WA figure at the date of the award. Asserted.
11. **Family care counts only above Amount B.** Care that would have been given
    anyway earns nothing (s 12(2)). Otherwise the care is capped at the all-employee
    average weekly earnings, or one-fortieth of it an hour under 40 hours. If the
    capped amount is Amount B or less, nothing (s 12(3)). Asserted.
12. **Drunk means presumed partly to blame.** If intoxicated, the injured person is
    presumed contributorily negligent unless they prove the intoxication "did not
    contribute in any way", or the court finds it was not self-induced (s 5L). How
    much the damages fall is for the 1947 contributory negligence Act. Asserted.
13. **Obvious risks of dangerous recreation defeat the claim, aware or not**
    (s 5H(2)). The exceptions are asking the defendant about the risk, or a written
    law requiring a warning. Asserted.
14. **A warning to a parent binds a 16- or 17-year-old, not a 15-year-old.**
    "child" in s 5I is only 16 or 17. Anyone under 18 is an "incompetent person" to
    whom a warning cannot be relied on. So for a child under 16 no risk warning
    defeats the claim under s 5I. Asserted.
15. **An "own risk" term in a recreational services contract excludes the claim**
    (s 5J(3)), unless the harm came from breach of a personal safety law or reckless
    disregard. Asserted.
16. **The Act creates no claim.** It only limits claims the general law gives
    (s 8, save Part 2A). Not asserted.
