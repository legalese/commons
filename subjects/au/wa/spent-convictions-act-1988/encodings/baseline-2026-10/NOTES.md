## Seat

**The person with an old matter: "Is it spent, and must I disclose it?"**
The Act is applied to the person who was convicted or charged: whether and when the conviction can be spent, what being spent does for them, and where that stops.
Every exported question is phrased as that person would ask it ("What kind of conviction is mine?", "From what day may I apply?", "Must I disclose my old matter when asked?", "Is it unlawful for them to treat me less favourably?").
Provisions addressed to the Commissioner of Police, the District Court judge, employers, licensing authorities and record keepers are encoded as what they mean for the person. That covers whether the Commissioner must issue the certificate (s 7(3)), whether the hearing is private (Schedule 1 cl 3), whether they can be made to pay costs (cl 8), and who may look the matter up (s 28).

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers.** Followed. The regulations that may prescribe a minor-punishment amount and the s 28(2) persons and laws are not deposited. The Act's own $100 is used, and "prescribed" under s 28(2) is an input.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the person, the Commissioner of Police, the District Court judge, employers, principals, organisations of employees and employers, licensing authorities, employment agencies, and the persons Schedule 3 names.
- **A judgment handed to someone else is answered conditionally.** Followed. These are asked as facts:
  - the judge's discretion under s 6(4), as whether the judge has ordered the conviction spent;
  - whether a requirement "is not reasonable" (s 17(2)(b)), a judgment for the Equal Opportunity Commission or Tribunal;
  - whether a person has a "lawful reason" (s 28(1));
  - the judge's view of costs (Schedule 1 cl 8(2)(b)) and of a public hearing (cl 3(1)(b)).
- **The Act's own definitions worked out, borrowed terms asked.** Followed, with one departure. Worked out from facts:
  - "serious conviction" (s 9) and "lesser conviction" (s 10), including the s 10(2) conversion;
  - "minor punishment" (s 3);
  - "prescribed period" (s 11, with s 32);
  - "spent conviction" (s 3), from the certificate, the order, the Sentencing Act 1995 s 39 order or Schedule 2.

  Borrowed terms are asked:
  - whether a non-WA conviction is spent under its own law (Schedule 2);
  - whether a youth conviction has stopped counting (Young Offenders Act 1994 s 189, encoded in that Act's own baseline).

  The departure is Schedule 3. It is the Act's own, but its table runs to about 90 roles. Twenty-two are encoded as data, chosen as the ones an ordinary person most often meets: police, prisons, security, firearms, schools, teachers, child care, health, disability services, the legal profession, and the two screening checks. The role is an input chosen from them.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** Followed: one conviction (`My conviction`), one matter (`My matter`: a conviction, a charge or a dismissal).
- **The Act's own terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed in comments. Field names use the person's words ("the Commissioner of Police has issued me a certificate that it is spent"), each tied to its section.
- **Operative sections in; machinery out.** Followed. Left out:
  - s 29 (the Equal Opportunity Act powers);
  - s 31 (prerogative of mercy);
  - s 33 (regulations);
  - Schedule 1 cll 2, 4-6 and 9 (parties, evidence, powers, sending the order);
  - the Schedule 3 exceptions for review boards, Department of Justice officers and the screening CEOs (cl 1(1) items 1-1B, 10-10B, 12-15; cl 2(7), (8); cl 3(2), (3)).

|---|---|---|---|
| k036 | Jake, 20 November 2026, aged 20: five years after the shop theft that went to a juvenile justice team | Does a team outcome leave anything to spend? | `What does Part 3 protect in my matter` (nothing: no conviction and no charge in court). | k082 | Jake, 4 March 2047, aged 40: a job form asks about convictions; the police certificate is blank | Must he disclose the 2021 matter? | `Must I disclose it` (nothing for this Act to act on), both with no Schedule 3 role and in his own line of work, a security licence. The scenario does not say what job the 2047 form is for. From 2034 Jake holds a security officer's licence (scenario chapter 6), so the security-licence row is asserted as well as the no-role row. The answer is the same either way.

This is consistent with the Young Offenders Act 1994 baseline. That encoding found that ss 189-190 of that Act stop a youth conviction counting and apply this Act's Part 3 to it "as if it were a spent conviction" (s 189(9)). Here, s 4(1)(a) takes such a conviction out of ss 6 and 7. `How can my conviction become spent` says it stops counting under the Young Offenders Act. `What does Part 3 protect in my matter` says Part 3 treats it as spent. `Must I disclose it` gives s 27(1)'s answer. Both encodings say Jake's team matter left no conviction.

## Why this scope

The plan entry names s 3, Part 2 and Part 3. Its seat asks whether a conviction is spent, what that means, and where disclosure is still required.
So the encoding covers:

- all of Part 2 (ss 6-11), with s 4 (what Part 2 does not reach) and s 32 (old convictions);
- all of Part 3's operative provisions: s 12 (what is treated as spent), ss 14-15 (where the protection stops), ss 17-24 (discrimination), ss 25-28 (disclosure, other laws, character, records);
- Schedule 3 as data, for the roles encoded;
- s 30, and the parts of Schedule 1 that affect the applicant.

Not encoded:

- the Schedule 3 roles not listed above, and the extensions to the other side of each relationship beyond what the comments note;
- s 3(2)(c) (how a non-WA sentence corresponds to a WA one; the person states the sentence that way);
- the Equal Opportunity Act 1984 procedure that a s 24 complaint uses;
- Part 4 machinery.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **A lesser conviction is not spent automatically after 10 years.** s 7(1): it "becomes spent when, on application being made in the prescribed form ... to the Commissioner of Police, the Commissioner issues to the applicant a certificate". After the period has run, the person must apply. But s 7(3) leaves the Commissioner no discretion: he "must issue a certificate if the application conforms with this Act". Asserted (a $500 fine is not spent without the certificate; with it, it is).
2. **A serious conviction needs a judge, and a refusal bars a new application for 2 years.** s 6(1), (2)(b), (4). The order is "at the discretion of the judge", having regard to seven listed matters, including "whether there is any public interest to be served in not making an order". Asserted (the route and the 2-year bar, both sides of it).
3. **"Serious" turns on what was imposed: more than a year's imprisonment, an indeterminate term, or a fine of $15 000 or more.** s 9. Exactly 12 months is lesser. Life imprisonment can never be spent under ss 6 or 7 (s 4(2)). s 10(2): a lesser conviction that is still unspent becomes serious if a later conviction brings a s 9 sentence. Asserted.
4. **The 10 years count from conviction, and the term imposed is added, whatever was served.** s 11(3). For an indeterminate term, s 11(2) starts the 10 years at discharge *and* adds the time actually served. Read literally, that counts the time inside twice. The encoding follows the words (INFERENCE as to purpose). Asserted (discharged 1 January 2020 after 180 months: first application 1 January 2045).
5. **Some cannabis convictions take 3 years, but only from 1 August 2011.** s 11(6), with the compilation table's commencement date for the Cannabis Law Reform Act 2010 Pt 4. Asserted.
6. **A later conviction while one is unspent restarts the clock for both, unless it carried only a fine of $100 or less.** s 11(4), (5) and s 3 "minor punishment". The period becomes the longest of the periods and runs from the latest conviction. Under s 11(4)(b), a cumulative sentence is added on top; applying (b) after (a) is a reading. Asserted (a $1 000 fine restarts; a $100 fine does not; 24 months cumulative gives 1 January 2029).
7. **A conviction before 1 July 1992 could not be spent before that day.** s 32(2). Asserted.
8. **Interstate convictions are spent only under Queensland, Commonwealth (and Norfolk Island) and New South Wales law.** s 8 and Schedule 2. Schedule 2 names no law of Victoria, South Australia, Tasmania or either Territory. So, read on its words, a conviction spent under one of those laws is not spent under this Act. Asserted.
9. **A youth conviction is outside Part 2 but inside Part 3.** s 4(1)(a) takes a conviction under Young Offenders Act 1994 s 189 out of ss 6 and 7. That Act's s 189(9) applies Part 3 to it as if it were spent. This matches the Young Offenders Act baseline. Asserted.
10. **A withdrawn charge, or one ending without a conviction, is protected at once.** s 12(b): Part 3 applies "as if the ... charge were a spent conviction", with no waiting period. The same goes for old Criminal Code s 669(1)(a) and Child Welfare Act dismissals and deemed non-convictions (s 12(a), (ab), (ac)). Asserted.
11. **Nothing in the Act reaches a matter that never came to court.** s 12 speaks of "a charge formally made in court". A caution or a juvenile justice team produces neither a conviction nor such a charge. So the Act has nothing to spend and nothing to protect. That a job form's question about convictions does not reach such a matter follows from the question's own words, not from this Act (INFERENCE). This is Jake's k036 and k082. Asserted.
12. **Being spent means a question about convictions is taken not to relate to it.** s 27(1): "Questions about a convicted person ... shall not be taken to relate to a spent conviction". s 27(2) applies the same to agreements and to rules of common law and equity, and s 25(2) to written laws requiring disclosure. Asserted.
13. **The protection stops in court, at bail, and in the person's own application.** s 14(1)(a), (2)(a): ss 25(2), 26(1) and 27 do not apply before a court or tribunal that applies the laws of evidence, or in s 6 proceedings. Schedule 1 cl 1(1)(a) requires the application to set out "all previous convictions", spent ones included (INFERENCE from s 14(2)(a)). s 15 excludes bail decisions. Asserted (court and application).
14. **Schedule 3 takes the protection away role by role, and the role decides which parts go.** Examples:
    - a security licence (cl 1(1) item 8) loses s 22 and Division 4 but keeps s 18;
    - a police officer (item 3) loses s 18 and Division 4;
    - a prison officer (item 5) loses ss 18, 19, 20, 22 and Division 4.

    The exceptions extend to the employer or authority on the other side (cl 1(2)). So a security employer's question is not protected by s 27, but the employer still may not discriminate under s 18. Asserted.
15. **The screening checks lose ss 27 and 28, not all of Division 4.** A person screened under the Working with Children (Screening) Act 2004 s 34 is excepted only from ss 27 and 28 (cl 2(6) item 1), and an NDIS worker screening likewise (cl 3(1) item 1). s 25(2) (written laws) and s 26 (character tests) still protect them. Asserted (a written law's question: still protected; a question: not; a character test: still protected).
16. **The child-care, safety-house and sport exceptions reach only offences against children, or sexual or morality offences.** Schedule 3 cl 2(2), (3). A spent theft conviction stays protected for a child-care job; a spent sexual offence, or an assault on a child, does not. Asserted.
17. **Schedule 3 never excepts ss 21 or 23.** No row of the table names s 21 (organisations of employees and employers) or s 23 (employment agencies). So those protections hold in every role. Asserted (a prison officer and an employment agency).
18. **The only remedy for discrimination is a complaint under the Equal Opportunity Act 1984.** s 24(1), (2): no other sanction, "criminal or civil"; s 24(3): defamation is not affected. s 26(2): ignoring the character rule "is not an offence". Comment only.
19. **Looking up a spent conviction without lawful reason costs up to $1 000.** s 28(1). There are exceptions for prescribed screening (s 28(2)) and for the Schedule 3 roles that lose s 28. Asserted.
20. **A spent conviction is not revived when parole or early release is cancelled.** s 30. Asserted.
21. **The application to the judge is heard in private unless the applicant asks or the judge decides otherwise, and each side bears its own costs.** Schedule 1 cll 3(1), 8(1). Costs may be awarded only after a refusal as vexatious, misconceived or lacking in substance, or where the judge thinks the circumstances justify it. Asserted.
22. **The adoption exception appears twice.** Schedule 3 cl 1(5) item 6 (all spent convictions) and cl 2(1) item 7 (only the cl 2(2) offences) both name applicants under the Adoption Act 1994 s 38(1), each for Division 4. The wider row governs. Only the cl 1(5) row is encoded.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- The rest of Schedule 3 encoded as data, together with the "other side" extensions in cl 1(2), (4), (6), (8), (10), (11), (13) and cl 2(4), (5).
- The Spent Convictions Regulations 1992 deposited. They hold the prescribed form, any prescribed minor-punishment amount, and the s 28(2) persons and laws.
- Whether the literal reading of s 11(2) (10 years from discharge plus time served) is the one applied in practice needs a decided case or the Commissioner's practice.
- The order of s 11(4)(a) and (b) for cumulative sentences is a reading, not settled.
- How Schedule 2's absence of Victoria, South Australia, Tasmania and the Territories is handled in practice needs checking against those jurisdictions' own laws and any later Schedule 2 amendments.
- The 29th-to-31st day edge cases in the month arithmetic are not handled.
