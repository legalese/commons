# Biosecurity Act 2015 — baseline encoding

## Method

**Baseline.** Written straight from the deposited text with the `writing-l4-rules`
skill. It is written from the seat of the individual to whom the law is applied,
with the design defaults ruled on 2026-10-18. No REFUSE. No pipeline, no coverage
table, no independent test pass, no human gate. Not for public use as advice.

Every decision is exported for Legalese Cloud with `@export`, and each input has a
`@desc`. There was no earlier encoding of this Act to replace.

The Act runs to two volumes and 645 sections. This encoding keeps to the incoming
traveller, as the brief asked:

- what the traveller must declare;
- what may not come in;
- what happens to goods that should not have come in, or were not declared;
- the penalties and infringement notices for not declaring or for a false answer;
- visa cancellation, as far as the text says anything about it.

Files: `ba-traveller.l4` (the module) and `ba-cases-traveller.l4` (the cases,
generated by the scratchpad script `gen_biosecurity-act-2015_baseline.py`).

## Edition

- **The Act:** Compilation No. 21, compilation date 16 September 2026, "Includes
  amendments: Act No. 84, 2026" (register id C2026C00403, two volumes). Deposited
  at `../../registers/source-bundle/C2015A00061.txt` (retrieved 2026-10-13).
- **Regulatory Powers (Standard Provisions) Act 2014:** Compilation No. 4,
  compilation date 20 March 2024. Deposited at
  `subjects/au/regulatory-powers-standard-provisions-act-2014/registers/source-bundle/C2014A00093.txt`.
  Used:
  - s 82(5): an individual's civil penalty is at most the penalty specified;
  - s 84(2): only one pecuniary penalty for the same conduct;
  - s 103(5): a notice for conduct that is both an offence and a civil penalty
    contravention relates to the offence.
- **Crimes Act 1914 s 4AA:** the penalty unit is deposited, but the indexed figure
  is not, so a penalty unit is an **input** (330 dollars in the illustrative cases).
- **Not deposited, and therefore inputs:**
  - the goods determinations under ss 173, 174 and 182, including the Biosecurity
    (Conditionally Non-prohibited Goods) Determination 2021 that the plan names.
    Whether a good is prohibited, suspended or conditional, and whether its
    conditions were met, are inputs;
  - the Biosecurity Regulation 2016: the "prescribed class of persons" in
    s 196(1)(b), any shorter payment period (s 524(2)(b)) and any prescribed notice
    amount (s 524(4)(c));
  - the approved form of the incoming passenger declaration, which sets which
    questions are asked;
  - any s 196A entry-requirement determination;
  - the Quarantine Act 1908, which applied in 2011.

## Seat

**The incoming traveller**: an individual entering Australian territory on an
incoming aircraft or vessel, with goods. The plan puts the question from that seat:
"What must I declare, and what may not come in?"

Almost every provision speaks to someone else:

- the Director of Biosecurity (ss 173, 174, 182, 196, 628);
- a biosecurity officer (ss 126, 131, 136);
- a court, or an infringement officer (ss 520, 523, 524).

Each is turned into what it means for the traveller: must I fill in the
declaration, may my goods come in, can they be taken or destroyed, what is the most
I can be made to pay, and what would a notice ask of me.

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers.** Followed. The
  Regulatory Powers Act is brought in because s 519 and s 523 apply its Parts 4 and
  5. The determinations and the Regulation are not deposited, so what they say is
  asked as an input.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the traveller, the Director of
  Biosecurity, a biosecurity officer, the court.
- **A judgment the law hands to someone else is answered conditionally.** Followed.
  These are all inputs:
  - the officer "suspects, on reasonable grounds" (ss 126, 131);
  - the officer "considers, on reasonable grounds" the goods cannot be treated
    (s 136);
  - "substantially the same" conduct (s 520).
- **The Act's own definitions worked out from facts; borrowed terms asked as
  facts.** Followed. "Prohibited goods", "suspended goods", "conditionally
  non-prohibited goods" and "subject to biosecurity control" are worked out from
  which determination applies and from the flight. "Material particular",
  "concealed" and "high-value goods" are asked.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** One arrival: the traveller, their goods
  and their answers.
- **The Act's own terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed, in `@desc`.
- **Operative sections in; machinery out.** Followed. Left out: the Directors'
  appointment, the BIRA process, determination-making procedure, permits
  administration and the regulation-making powers.

## The lives

- **Chloe c014 (2011, age 8): home from Bali.** The passenger card, and nothing to
  declare for biosecurity. Decisions asked:
  - `Did the Act apply when I arrived`: **no**. The Act commenced on 16 June 2016
    (s 2). The day is assumed to be 1 July 2011, since the life gives only the year.
  - On the 2026 text:
    - `Must I fill in the biosecurity declaration`: yes (assumed: she is in the
      prescribed class and was required);
    - `Do I have anything to declare for biosecurity`: no;
    - `Have I failed to declare my goods`: no;
    - `Are my goods under biosecurity control`: yes, until released;
    - `May my goods come in`: yes, no determination catches her souvenirs
      (assumed);
    - `Can my goods be forfeited`: no;
    - `Am I liable for a false answer`: no;
    - `Does this Act provide for my visa to be cancelled`: no.

  The illustrative variants (a Bali traveller with beef jerky, ticking NO or YES)
  show what the event would turn on if there had been something to declare.

The customs part of the same event belongs to the Customs Act 1901 baseline. So does
Jake's false card (k046); the plan does not cite this Act for it.

## Why this scope

The Act is mostly about cargo, conveyances, ballast water, approved arrangements,
emergencies and officers' powers. What reaches a person walking through the
arrivals hall comes from a few places:

- the goods are under biosecurity control from the moment the aircraft enters
  Australian territory (s 119);
- the traveller must give the information required on the declaration (s 196) and
  meet any entry requirement (s 196C);
- whether goods may come in depends on determinations (ss 173, 174, 182);
- goods that should not have come in can be forfeited (s 628), and any risky goods
  can be destroyed (s 136);
- there are contraventions for bringing goods in (ss 185, 186, 186A) and for false
  answers (s 532), most of them open to infringement notices (ss 523, 524).

Left out:

- the commercial-advantage and environmental-harm offences (ss 185(4)-(5),
  186(4)-(5));
- possession offences (s 188);
- permits (Part 3 Division 3) beyond "a condition was not met";
- biosecurity measures other than destruction, and review of destruction;
- human biosecurity (Chapter 2);
- Criminal Code ss 137.1 and 137.2 (not deposited).

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **The Act did not exist when Chloe came home.** Sections 3 to 645 commenced on
   16 June 2016 (s 2, item 2). Her 2011 return fell under the Quarantine Act 1908,
   which is not deposited. Asserted.
2. **The duty to declare is not in the Act alone.** Section 196 applies only to
   persons "included in a prescribed class of persons", and only once the Director
   "require[s]" the information, "in the manner approved". The class is in the
   Regulation and the questions are in the approved form; neither is deposited.
   Failing to comply carries a civil penalty of 120 penalty units (s 196(4)).
   Asserted.
3. **You cannot refuse to answer on the ground of self-incrimination, but your
   answer is protected.** Section 635(1) removes the privilege for ss 126, 196 and
   196C. Section 635(2) makes the answer inadmissible against an individual, except
   in proceedings for that answer being false (Criminal Code ss 137.1, 137.2; ss 532,
   533). Asserted.
4. **A false declaration is a civil penalty only if you knew, and only if you were
   warned.** Section 532 needs the person to know the information is false or
   misleading, in a material particular. It does not apply if the official "did not
   take reasonable steps to inform the person" that a civil penalty may follow
   (s 532(4)). The words "You may be liable to a civil penalty for giving false or
   misleading information" are enough (s 532(5)). Maximum 600 penalty units.
   Asserted.
5. **Suspension beats conditions.** Under s 182(7), a suspension determination
   prevails over an inconsistent s 174 determination. So conditional goods that are
   suspended cannot come in even if every condition is met, and bringing them in
   falls under s 185, not s 186. Asserted.
6. **Forfeiture does not need fault.** Section 628 applies to goods that were
   prohibited, suspended, or conditional with an applicable condition unmet. It
   applies even where the traveller is outside s 186 because someone else failed the
   condition (s 186(7)). A failure to notify does not affect the forfeiture
   (s 628(6)). Asserted.
7. **Concealment has its own penalty and a fixed notice.** Section 186A: 1,200
   penalty units for concealed conditional goods. An infringement notice for it is
   20 penalty units for an individual "despite subsection (4)" (s 524(4A)).
   Asserted.
8. **An infringement notice is at most 12 penalty units otherwise, and the
   regulations can only lower it.** Section 524(4) sets the amount as "the least of"
   one-fifth of the court maximum, 12 penalty units (individual), and any number the
   regulations prescribe. Read literally, a prescribed number counts only when it is
   the least. That is a reading, labelled in the module; the Regulation is not
   deposited to see what it prescribes. Asserted on that reading.
9. **A notice for prohibited goods is for the strict liability offence.** The
   s 523(1) table lists s 185(2A) but not s 185(2) or (3). Regulatory Powers Act
   s 103(5) also requires a notice to relate to the offence where the conduct is both
   an offence and a civil penalty contravention. The fine for s 185(2A) is 60 penalty
   units, so the notice is 12 penalty units. Asserted.
10. **The payment period can be the same day.** It is 28 days, or an earlier period
    the regulations prescribe, which the note says may end "on the day that the
    notice is given" (s 524(2)). Asserted.
11. **A customs conviction bars a biosecurity civil penalty for the same conduct.**
    Section 520(1): no civil penalty order if the person has been convicted, or found
    liable, under an Australian law for conduct that is the same or substantially the
    same. RPA s 84(2) allows only one pecuniary penalty for the same conduct. This is
    where the Customs Act baseline meets this one. The card carries two separate
    duties: customs (Customs Regulation 2015 reg 27) and biosecurity (s 196). A false
    answer to each is a separate statement; whether a court treats them as the
    "same conduct" is an input. Asserted (the bar).
12. **The Act says nothing about visas.** The word "visa" does not occur in the
    deposited compilation. Any visa consequence of a biosecurity contravention would
    come from the Migration Act 1958, which is not encoded here. Asserted (as "no").
13. **Destroying high-value goods needs the Director's written approval**
    (s 136(2)). Otherwise an officer may require destruction whenever goods under
    control carry an unacceptable risk and cannot be effectively treated, declared
    or not. Asserted.

  suspended). Then whether a good may come in could be worked out from the goods
  rather than asked.

  - the prescribed class for s 196;

    its text.

  must I declare" can be answered question by question.
