# Sale of Goods Act 1979 — baseline encoding

**Method: baseline.** Written straight from the deposited text with the `writing-l4-rules`
skill, from the seat of the individual to whom the law is applied, with the design defaults
ruled on 2026-10-18. No `REFUSE`: where the encoding does not cover a case it returns a
constructor named `not covered by this encoding: ...` or `NOTHING`, and says why in a
comment. No pipeline, no coverage table, no independent test pass, no human gate. Not for
public use as advice.

**Edition:** 2020 Revised Edition, informal consolidation, version in force from 1 April 2022,
as deposited at `../../registers/source-bundle/SGA1979.txt`. The cover says the revised
edition "incorporates all amendments up to and including 1 December 2021" and came into
operation on 31 December 2021. The latest amendment annotated is Act 25 of 2021, wef
1 April 2022 (ss 52 and 61, "claimant").

**Checks:** one module (`soga-buyers.l4`) and one case file (`soga-cases-buyers.l4`), run
with the stable `l4` of VS Code extension 1.6.98: 60 assertions written, 60 satisfied,
0 errors, 0 warnings. The case file is generated by `gen_sale-of-goods-act-1979_baseline.py`
(scratchpad), with expected values written by hand from the text. A deliberately wrong
expected value was tried and the runner reported it as a failure.

This encoding replaces the first-pass encoding `naive-2026-10`. It covers everything that one
covered (ss 6, 11(3), 14, 15A, 18 Rule 4, 20, 21, 23, 30, 35, 50, 51, 53, 57) and reuses its
readings where they still held. No encoding of this Act was found among those moved to
`solutions/` on 2026-10-19.

## Seat

**The buyer**: the individual who "buys or agrees to buy goods" (s 61(1)). Most of the Act
is about the bargain between seller and buyer. The buyer is the person who asks what they can
do when the goods are faulty, late, short or not the seller's to sell, and what the seller can
make them pay. Every decision is phrased as the buyer asks it (`What can I do about the goods`,
`Have I accepted the goods`, `Do I own the goods I bought`, `Can the seller sue me for the
price`, `May I retract my bid`).

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers or dates brought in.** Followed.
  The Act sets no figures by subsidiary legislation. Its dates (1 January 1894, and 19 May 1985
  for the Schedule) come from the Act itself. The Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 (which defines
  "deals as consumer", s 61(4A)) and the Factors Act 1889 are other Acts and are not brought in.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: buyer, seller, the seller's agent (s 14(5)), the
  owner (ss 21 to 25) and the auctioneer (s 57).
- **A judgment handed to someone else answered conditionally.** Followed. Satisfactory quality
  ("a reasonable person would regard as satisfactory"), "so slight that it would be
  unreasonable", "a reasonable time" (s 59: "a question of fact"), "a reasonable price"
  (s 8(3)) and the s 37 loss and charge are all inputs, never guessed. Where s 15A and s 30(2A)
  put the burden on the seller, the input is "the seller has shown ...".
- **The Act's own definitions worked out, borrowed terms asked as facts.** Followed. Worked
  out: contract of sale (s 2(1)), "goods", "specific goods", "necessaries" (s 3(3)), the s 14(5)
  agent who counts as a business seller, acceptance (s 35), and the s 18 Rules. Asked as facts:
  "deals as consumer" (from the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977), and whether a title was void or
  voidable (general law, s 62(2)).
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** Followed: one contract of sale, one lot of goods.
- **The Act's own terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed in the comments, which quote the
  statutory words above each `§§` ("property" is ownership, "risk" is who bears an accidental
  loss, "warranty" is a lesser term that gives damages only).
- **Operative sections in scope, machinery out.** Followed, with one departure. Part 5 (the
  unpaid seller's lien, stoppage in transit and re-sale) is operative, but it addresses what the
  seller may do against the goods. It is left out for size, not as machinery. Specific
  performance (s 52) is a discretion of the court and is left out.

## Why this scope

These are the provisions a buyer meets. Does the Act reach my purchase, and what must I pay
(ss 1, 2, 3(2), 8, 62(4))? What if the goods had perished (ss 6, 7)? What does the seller
promise about title and about the goods (ss 12 to 15)? May I still reject, or only claim
damages (ss 11(3), 15A, 35, 53(1))? When do the goods become mine and at my risk (ss 17, 18,
20)? Do I own goods bought from someone who did not own them (ss 21, 23 to 25)? What about a
short or excess delivery (s 30), and do I have to send rejected goods back (s 36)? What can
the seller claim from me (ss 37, 49, 50), what can I claim (ss 51, 53), and how do auctions
work (s 57)?

Left out: capacity beyond necessaries, formalities, future goods and valuation (ss 3(1), 4,
5, 9), time stipulations (s 10), waiver (s 11(1)), the credit-broker limb (s 14(3)(b)),
usage (s 14(4)), reservation of the right of disposal (s 19), bulk goods (ss 20A, 20B),
delivery rules (ss 27 to 29, 31 to 33), commercial units (s 35(7)), partial rejection
(s 35A), Part 5, specific performance (s 52), interest (s 54), exclusion of implied terms
(s 55), conflict of laws (s 56), and the Schedule's modified sections. A contract made before
19 May 1985 gets an explicit "not covered" answer.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **Only a business seller owes satisfactory quality and fitness, but a business agent can
   make a private sale a business one.** s 14(1): otherwise "there is no implied condition or
   warranty about the quality or fitness". A consignment shop selling for a private owner owes
   both, unless the buyer knew the owner was private or reasonable steps were taken to tell
   them (s 14(5)). Asserted.
2. **A private seller still owes correspondence with the description.** s 13(1) is not limited
   to sales in the course of a business. A car a private seller advertises as a 2020 model that
   turns out to be a 2016 model can be rejected. Asserted.
3. **A defect pointed out, one a pre-contract inspection ought to have revealed, or one the
   sample would have shown is outside the quality condition** (s 14(2C)(a) to (c)). In a sale by
   sample, s 15(2)(b) excludes the same defect. Asserted.
4. **Fitness for a stated purpose fails where the buyer did not rely on the seller,** or it was
   unreasonable to (s 14(3)). Asserted.
5. **A consumer may reject for even a slight breach; a buyer who does not deal as consumer may
   not.** s 15A(1) turns a slight breach into a breach of warranty only "if the buyer does not
   deal as consumer". The seller must show both the slightness (s 15A(3)) and that the buyer is
   not a consumer (s 61(4B)). s 30(2A) and (2B) do the same for short and excess deliveries.
   Asserted.
6. **Acceptance ends rejection, not damages.** After acceptance a breach of condition "can only
   be treated as a breach of warranty" (s 11(3)), unless the contract says otherwise. The buyer
   may then set the loss against the price "in diminution or extinction" of it, or sue for
   damages (s 53(1)). A severable contract the buyer has partly accepted turns on s 31(2) and is
   not covered. Asserted.
7. **Asking for a repair is not acceptance, and a consumer cannot sign away the chance to
   examine.** s 35(6)(a), (3). Using the goods before any chance to examine them is not yet
   acceptance (s 35(2)). Keeping them beyond a reasonable time is (s 35(4)). Asserted.
8. **Property passes when the parties intend; the Rules only fill the gap.** Under an
   unconditional contract for specific goods in a deliverable state, property passes when the
   contract is made (Rule 1). Where the seller must still do something to the goods, it passes
   only once that is done and the buyer has notice (Rule 2). Goods on approval become the
   buyer's once the buyer keeps them past the return time (Rule 4). A retention-of-title
   agreement is a different intention: s 17 governs, and the encoding says it is not covered.
   Asserted.
9. **Risk follows property, except that the party whose fault delayed delivery bears the loss
   the delay caused** (s 20(1), (2)). Agreement otherwise returns `NOTHING`. Asserted.
10. **An honest buyer from a thief gets nothing. An honest buyer from a fraudster, or from a
    seller who had already sold the goods but kept them, may get good title** (ss 21(1), 23,
    24). Good faith is honesty, "whether it is done negligently or not" (s 61(2)). s 25 takes
    its effect from the Factors Act 1889, which is not deposited, so that case is not covered.
    Asserted.
11. **A buyer need not send rightly rejected goods back.** Telling the seller is enough, unless
    otherwise agreed (s 36). Asserted.
12. **The seller can sue for the price only once property has passed, or where the price falls
    due on a fixed day whatever happens to delivery** (s 49). Asserted.
13. **The market measures of damages do not say which way round.** ss 50(3) and 51(3) give
    "the difference between the contract price and the market or current price". The direction
    and the floor at zero are an inference from "loss" in ss 50(2) and 51(2). s 53(3) is
    explicit: value as warranted less value at delivery. Asserted (with the inference).
14. **Necessaries: a minor, or someone incompetent through mental incapacity or drunkenness,
    must pay a reasonable price** for necessaries sold and delivered to them (s 3(2)). Otherwise
    capacity is left to the general law, and the encoding returns `NOTHING`. Asserted.
15. **The Act reaches only sales for money, of goods** (s 2(1), "goods" in s 61(1)), and not a
    sale meant as security (s 62(4)). Asserted.
16. **On request, the seller must give the buyer a reasonable opportunity to examine the goods
    when tendering delivery**, unless otherwise agreed (s 34). Encoded, not asserted.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- A Singapore commercial-law reader should check every assertion against the text, especially
  findings 6, 8 and 13, which rest on readings.
- "Deals as consumer" is defined by Part 1 of the Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977 (s 61(4A)),
  and exclusions of the implied terms are subject to it (s 55). That Act should be deposited and
  encoded, and the consumer question worked out from facts.
- The Consumer Protection (Fair Trading) Act 2003, Part 3, gives a consumer further remedies
  (repair, replacement, price reduction, rescission) that run alongside this Act. The two
  encodings are not linked.
- s 35A (partial rejection), s 35(7) (commercial units), severable contracts under s 31(2), the
  Schedule's modified sections, and Part 5 are not encoded.
- No case law was searched. "Satisfactory quality", "slight", "reasonable time" and
  "reasonable price" are facts supplied by the user.
