# Consumer Protection (Fair Trading) Act 2003 — baseline encoding

**Method: baseline.** Written straight from the deposited text with the `writing-l4-rules`
skill, from the seat of the individual to whom the law is applied, with the design defaults
ruled on 2026-10-18. No `REFUSE`: where the encoding does not cover a case it returns
`NOTHING` and says why in a comment. No pipeline, no coverage table, no independent test
pass, no human gate. Not for public use as advice.

**Edition:** 2020 Revised Edition, informal consolidation, version in force from 9 March 2025
(SSO "Current version as at 30 Sep 2026"), as deposited at
`../../registers/source-bundle/CPFTA2003.txt`.

**Checks:** two modules (`cpfta-unfair-practices.l4`, `cpfta-defective-goods.l4`) and two
case files, run with the stable `l4` of VS Code extension 1.6.98:
`cpfta-cases-unfair-practices.l4` 45 assertions, `cpfta-cases-defective-goods.l4` 15; 60
satisfied, 0 errors, 0 warnings. The case files are generated by
`gen_consumer-protection-fair-trading-act-2003_baseline.py` (scratchpad) with expected values
written by hand from the text.

This encoding replaces the one moved to `solutions/Litigate.4` on 2026-10-19. Its NOTES were
read for scope and pitfalls; none of its L4 was copied.

## Seat

**The consumer**: the individual who bought, hired, was given or won goods or services from
a business. In Part 3 the same person is the "transferee" (buyer, transferee or hirer). The
Act exists "to protect consumers against unfair practices and to give consumers additional
rights in respect of goods that do not conform to contract", so the consumer is the person
it is applied to. Every decision is phrased as the consumer asks it (`Am I a consumer`,
`Can I sue the supplier for an unfair practice`, `May I ask for a price reduction or rescind
the contract`, `Must I wait before rejecting the goods or demanding another remedy`).

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers brought in.** Followed. The
  Act itself states the $30,000 limit (s 6(6)); no Minister's order changing it is
  deposited, so the Act's figure is a named constant. No regulations under s 11 (cancellation),
  s 41(2) (burden of proof) or s 43(2)(k) (dispute-resolution schemes) are deposited, so those
  provisions are out and the encoding says so.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: consumer, supplier, transferee, transferor, the
  court, the Small Claims Tribunal. The transferor is called "the seller" in field names, as a
  plain gloss.
- **A judgment handed to someone else answered conditionally.** Followed. Disproportion of a
  remedy (s 15(3), (4)), incompatibility of the 6-month rule (s 14(4)(b)), the court's
  apportionment for part-business use (s 7(8)) and "a reasonable time" (ss 15(2), 17(1)) are
  inputs or named outcomes ("relief only for the part the court attributes ..."), never guessed.
- **The Act's own definitions worked out, borrowed terms asked as facts.** Followed:
  "consumer", "supplier", "consumer transaction" with the First Schedule, "unsolicited goods or
  services" and "applicable agreement" are worked out. "Deals as consumer" (borrowed from the
  Unfair Contract Terms Act 1977), conformity to the contract (Sale of Goods Act 1979, Supply
  of Goods Act 1982, Hire-Purchase Act 1969), and the Small Claims Tribunals Act Schedule are
  asked as facts.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** Followed: one consumer transaction, one claim, one
  set of goods.
- **The Act's own terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed in the comments, which quote the
  statutory words above each `§§`.
- **Operative sections in scope, machinery out.** Followed, with one departure: the
  Commission's own proceedings (ss 8 to 10, Parts 3A, 3B) are operative but address the
  supplier and the Commission, not the consumer; they are left out for size, not as machinery.

## Why this scope

The provisions a consumer meets: am I protected (s 2, First Schedule, s 3), was what the
supplier did unfair (s 4, Second Schedule), can I sue and for how much and until when (ss 6,
7, 12), can the contract take that away (s 35), and the lemon law for goods that turn out
faulty (Part 3). Of the 27 Second Schedule practices, four are worked out from facts the
consumer can state (para 10 price over estimate, paras 25 and 26 unsolicited goods, para 27
the refused copy); the other 23 are asked as one fact. Left out: voluntary compliance
agreements (s 8), the Commission's declarations and injunctions (ss 9, 10), cancellation
(s 11), stays (s 6(7) to (9)), the court's list of orders (s 7(5)), the dispute-resolution
factor (s 7(10), (11)), the Fourth Schedule disability extension, investigation and offences
(Parts 3A, 3B), publishers (s 38), burden of proof (s 41) and ss 42 to 44.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **Buying a home is not a consumer transaction; renting one is.** "Goods" includes "any
   residential property", even one being built, but the First Schedule excludes "acquisition
   of an estate or interest in any immovable property", except a lease of residential property
   for rent or a time share contract. Asserted.
2. **Paying for unsolicited goods does not make them solicited.** Second Schedule Part 2
   para 2: a request "is not to be inferred only from" payment, use or inaction. A demand for
   payment for goods the consumer never asked for is para 25, even after the consumer used
   them. An invoice for them is para 26 unless its most prominent text says it is not a bill.
   Asserted.
3. **A deposit counts as an instalment.** Para 27 covers only an "applicable agreement", one
   paid "by 2 or more instalments", and "any deposit ... is treated as an instalment". So a
   deposit and one payment qualify, and refusing a copy is an unfair practice; a single
   payment does not. Asserted.
4. **Taking advantage needs knowledge.** s 4(c) is unfair only if the supplier "knows or ought
   reasonably to know" the consumer cannot protect their interests or understand the
   transaction. A hard bargain without that is not s 4(c). Asserted.
5. **The $30,000 limit counts admitted set-off, and the excess can be given up.** A $35,000
   claim less $6,000 set-off admitted in the particulars is within the limit; a bare $35,000
   claim is not, unless the excess is abandoned, and then no more than $30,000 can be recovered
   in that action (s 6(3), (5)). Asserted.
6. **The value of a home under s 6(4) has a gap.** (b) takes the rent only "if this value is
   lower than the value in paragraph (a)". Where a rent is known but there is no annual value,
   nothing can be compared, and (c) applies only if all three are unascertainable (the reading
   taken). The encoding returns `NOTHING` there. Asserted.
7. **Two years from the later of the last event and the consumer's knowledge** (s 12(1)),
   with knowledge including what the consumer could reasonably have found out (s 12(7)).
   Starting on the second anniversary is in time; the next day is not. "2 years after" 29
   February is taken as 28 February: an inference, the text gives no rule. Asserted.
8. **No relief at all for goods meant for business use.** s 7(7) bars it, and s 7(9) counts
   goods bought to resell or to use up in production. Where use is mixed the court may
   apportion and grant relief for the non-business part only. Asserted.
9. **A spoken warranty can be proved against the written contract** (s 39, despite the
   Evidence Act), but not for goods or services intended for business use. Asserted.
10. **The lemon law presumes faults found within 6 months were there at delivery.** Part 3
    applies if the consumer deals as consumer, the goods did not conform at delivery, and the
    contract was made on or after 1 September 2012. A fault appearing within 6 months is taken
    to have been there at delivery, unless the seller establishes otherwise or the rule is
    incompatible with the goods. It is for the seller to show the buyer did not deal as
    consumer (s 13(3)). The 6 months are counted to the same day 6 months on, a missing day
    falling back to the month's end (31 August to 28 February): an inference. Asserted.
11. **Repair or replacement comes first.** A price reduction or rescission is open only if
    neither repair nor replacement may be required, or the seller was asked and failed to do
    it within a reasonable time without significant inconvenience (s 16(2)). A refund on
    rescission may be reduced for the use had (s 16(3)). Asserted.
12. **Having asked for a repair, the consumer must wait a reasonable time** before rejecting
    the goods or demanding another remedy (s 17). Read literally, s 17(2)(b) is the very demand
    s 17(1) presupposes; it is read as a further demand (an inference). Asserted.
13. **Part 4 does not apply to Part 3.** s 13(5)(c) disapplies "the provisions in Part 4", and
    Part 4 holds s 35 (no contracting out), s 39 (warranties) and s 40 (ambiguity read against
    the supplier). Read in the text, not asserted; what it means for a term excluding the
    lemon-law rights would turn on other Acts not deposited.
14. **There is no right to cancel until regulations create one.** s 11 only lets the Minister
    prescribe classes of cancellable contracts; none is deposited. Read, not asserted.
