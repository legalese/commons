## Seat

**The accused person**: the individual charged with an offence and tried in a Singapore court. The Evidence Act is
addressed almost entirely to the court ("the court is to presume", "the court may ask") and to witnesses, but the person
it is applied to, and who bears the cost of every rule, is the accused. Each decision is asked as the accused would ask
it: `Can I be made to give evidence`, `Must I answer a question about my past or my character`, `Must I prove this
fact`, `May my lawyer disclose it`. Where the accused is also a party to a civil suit (ss 54, 122(1)), the same person
is asked about.

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers or dates.** Followed. The Act sets no numbers here; no
  instrument was needed. The First Schedule (lists of child abuse and sexual offences) is part of the Act but is not
  worked out: whether an offence is "a child abuse offence" or "a sexual offence" is asked as a fact. Departure, for size.
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the accused, the prosecution, a person jointly charged, the wife or husband,
  the advocate and solicitor, the court.
- **A judgment handed to someone else answered conditionally.** Followed. The court's opinions in ss 122(7) and
  134(4)(b), and what "may appear to the court necessary" in s 131(1), are inputs named as the court's opinion.
- **The Act's own definitions worked out from facts, borrowed terms asked as facts.** Followed for "admission" and
  "confession" (s 17) and "specified offence" (s 124(3)). Borrowed Penal Code terms ("domestic worker", "vulnerable
  person", Chapter 16 offences) are asked as facts. The First Schedule departure is above.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** Followed: one question in cross-examination, one statement, one
  communication, one fact to be proved.
- **The Act's own terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed: field and constructor names keep the Act's words
  ("bad disposition or reputation", "in furtherance of an illegal purpose"); glosses are in comments.
- **Operative sections in scope, machinery out.** Followed, and further narrowed to the provisions an accused most meets;
  see the next section.

## Why this scope

The Act has 177 sections and two Schedules. An encoding of 25 to 60 assertions cannot hold it, so this one takes the
provisions that decide what happens to the accused personally: whether they must testify and what they can then be asked
(ss 122, 56(4), 134(4), (6)); what they must prove (ss 103, 105 to 108); what their own words count as (ss 17, 21); when
their character comes in (ss 54 to 56); and the two privileges that protect what they said in confidence (s 124 to a
spouse, ss 128, 130, 131 to a lawyer), with ss 135 and 136 on corroboration.

Left out: relevancy generally (ss 5 to 16, 32 to 53), proof of documents (ss 61 to 92), the parol evidence rule
(ss 93 to 102), the presumptions (ss 109 to 116A), estoppel (ss 117 to 119), the other privileges (ss 123, 125 to 127,
128A, 129, 132, 133), examination of witnesses (ss 137 to 169) and bankers' books (ss 170 to 176). The moved encoding
covered most of these; its notes record pitfalls in them (s 8 Explanations, s 49, s 80A, ss 70 and 71) that a later
encoding of those Parts should read.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **I cannot be made to give evidence at my own trial, but once I do, the shield against questions about my record has
   five ways out.** s 122(3) makes the accused competent but "not compellable". s 122(4) bars questions revealing other
   offences or bad disposition, except where the fact is admissible to prove the offence charged (5), to clear a
   co-accused (6), where I attacked a witness's record and the court thinks the main purpose was credibility (7), where I
   gave evidence against a co-accused (8), or where I put my good character in issue (s 56(4)). Even under (7), the court
   "is not to permit" the question unless it is relevant to my credibility. Asserted.
2. **Whether a party to a civil suit can be compelled is not said by s 122(1).** It says only that the parties are
   "competent witnesses"; the encoding answers that case as not covered. Asserted.
3. **The burden of the Penal Code's exceptions is on me, and the court starts by presuming them absent.** s 107 puts
   the burden of a general exception, or a special exception or proviso, on the accused "and the court is to presume the
   absence of such circumstances". An alibi is also mine to prove (s 105, illustration (b)), as is a fact especially
   within my knowledge (s 108). That I committed the offence, and that I admitted it, are the prosecution's (s 103
   illustration (a), s 105 illustration (a)). Asserted.
4. **A confession is any admission by an accused suggesting they committed the offence, made "at any time"; whether it is
   voluntary is not in this Act.** s 17(2). Sections 24 to 30 are "[Repealed by Act 15 of 2010]", so the admissibility
   of a confession is governed elsewhere (the Criminal Procedure Code 2010, by inference: that Code was not read here).
   Asserted for the definition.
5. **I cannot prove my own admission in my favour except in three cases.** s 21(a) to (c): if it would be relevant
   under s 32 were I dead (illustration (c), a letter dated at Penang), a contemporaneous statement of state of mind or
   body backed by conduct, or relevance otherwise than as an admission. Asserted.
6. **Putting my good character in issue lets in my previous convictions.** s 55 makes good character relevant in
   criminal proceedings; s 56(2) and (3) let the prosecution or a co-accused prove bad disposition or reputation and
   "previous convictions, if any" once I have asked, given or called evidence of good character. In a civil case,
   character to prove conduct is irrelevant unless it appears from facts otherwise relevant (s 54). Asserted.
7. **My spouse's privilege falls away for "specified offences", and a child abuse offence against an adult domestic
   worker is not one.** s 124(2), (3): limb (a) needs a victim below 16; limbs (c) and (d) cover only sexual and
   Chapter 16 offences. Asserted.
8. **Section 124(1)'s exception for proceedings between spouses follows the permission limb, and the text does not say
   whether it also lifts the compulsion limb.** The encoding answers that case as not covered, and lets the spouse
   disclose. Asserted.
9. **Telling my solicitor I committed the crime is protected; using my solicitor to commit one is not; and giving
   evidence myself is not a waiver.** s 128 illustrations (a) to (c); s 130(1); calling the solicitor and questioning
   them on protected matters is deemed consent (s 130(2)). A fact the lawyer merely observed is not among the things
   s 128(1) protects (an inference from its list). Asserted.
10. **In the witness box I cannot refuse to answer because it would prove the offence charged, and the credibility
    exception in s 134(4)(b) takes nothing away but grants nothing either.** Whether a right to refuse survives in that
    case is answered as not covered (s 134(1) says a witness "is not excused" generally). A compelled answer under
    (4)(b) cannot be proved against me in proceedings for another offence (s 134(6)); (6) is silent on answers
    compelled under (4)(a), answered `NOTHING`. Asserted.
11. **No corroboration and no second witness is required, and the accomplice warning is abrogated.** ss 135(2), 136.
    Asserted.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- The First Schedule's offence lists should be encoded so that "child abuse offence" and "sexual offence" are worked out
  rather than asked.
- The Criminal Procedure Code 2010's rules on the voluntariness of statements (by inference, ss 258 and following) and
  CPC ss 265 and 266 (cited by s 122(5)) should be read with ss 17, 21 and 122.
- The two open readings (s 124(1)'s exception and the compulsion limb; whether a right to refuse survives the
  s 134(4)(b) exception) want a domain expert or decided cases; none were searched.
- The rest of the Act (see "Why this scope") is not encoded.
- No independent test pass has been run.
