# Computer Misuse Act 1993 — baseline encoding

**Method: baseline.** Written straight from the deposited text with the `writing-l4-rules`
skill, from the seat of the individual to whom the law is applied, with the design defaults
ruled on 2026-10-18. No `REFUSE` (the stable binary rejects it). No pipeline, no coverage
table, no independent test pass, no human gate. Not for public use as advice.

**Edition:** 2020 Revised Edition, informal consolidation, version in force from 30/12/2025
(amendments to Act 21 of 2025), as deposited at `../../registers/source-bundle/CMA1993.txt`.

**Checks:** one module (`cma-offences.l4`) and one case file (`cma-cases-offences.l4`):
60 assertions written, 60 satisfied, 0 errors, 0 warnings, with the stable `l4` of VS Code
extension 1.6.98. Expected values were written by hand from the text; the fixtures were
generated by a script in the session scratchpad.

This replaces the encoding moved to `solutions/Litigate.4` on 2026-10-19. That encoding's
notes were read for scope and pitfalls; none of its L4 was copied.

## Seat

**The accused person**: an individual whose conduct with a computer, a password or a
Singpass credential is in question. The Act is a criminal statute, and the person it is
applied to is the one who did, or is said to have done, the act. Every decision is asked as
that person would ask it (`Have I committed the section 3 offence`, `What is the most I can
be punished with`, `Can a police officer arrest me without a warrant`), and the facts are
ones that person could state about their own conduct.

The 2026-10-18 defaults:

- **The Act alone, with the instruments that set its numbers.** Followed. No instrument is
  deposited, so three numbers or lists the Minister may prescribe are inputs, and the module
  says so: a different damage threshold (s 2(1) "damage" (a); `NOTHING` means $10,000), an
  excluded device (s 2(1) "computer" (d)), and the compoundable offences (s 16(2)).
- **The actors the text names.** Followed: the accused, the court, the Commissioner of
  Police (s 16), a police officer (s 19), the victim (s 17).
- **A judgment handed to someone else, answered conditionally.** Followed. What the court is
  satisfied of (rebutting the s 8A(3) and s 11(3) presumptions, scam use and reasonable steps
  under ss 8A(5) and 8B(5B), damage), the officer's reasonable suspicion (s 19) and the
  Commissioner's choice to compound (s 16) are inputs named as such. s 16 answers only the
  most that can be collected if composition is offered.
- **The Act's own definitions worked out from facts; borrowed terms asked as facts.**
  Followed for "computer", "secures access", "without authority", "unauthorised
  modification", "damage", "credential" (First Schedule), "protected computer" and "serious
  harm in Singapore" (glossed; asked as one fact). "Scam offence" (Second Schedule, via the
  Penal Code) and "offence under any written law" are borrowed and asked as facts.
- **One case is the text's natural unit.** Followed: one act or one conviction. s 14 takes a
  set of alleged acts because that is its unit.
- **The Act's own terms, each with a plain gloss.** Followed in comments above each rule.
- **Operative sections in; machinery out.** Followed: ss 1, 15, 18 and 20 are out. s 15
  (the lower courts may impose the full penalty) changes nothing the person computes.

One departure: the punishment rule is **dated**. Only the text in force from 30 December
2025 is deposited, and the caning limbs of ss 8A and 8B came in that day, so conduct before
it gets `not covered by this encoding` rather than a guess about the earlier text. The other
rules take no date.

## Why this scope

The individual most meets the offences, what a conviction can cost, whether the Act reaches
conduct abroad, and what police and the court can do to them. The elements of ss 3, 4, 5,
7, 8, 8A, 8B, 9 and 10 are encoded; s 6 (use or interception of a computer service) has its
punishment encoded but not its elements. s 14(4) (what a charge must say), s 17(3) (civil
debt) and s 12(2) (place immaterial) are noted, not computed. The Act lays prohibitions on
the individual, not duties with deadlines, so no regulative rule is written.

## What the Act turns out to say

1. **Section 4 does not care whether the access was authorised.** s 4(4)(a): it is
   "immaterial whether ... the access ... is authorised or unauthorised". The rule has no
   authority input at all. The intended offence must involve property, fraud, dishonesty or
   bodily harm and carry at least 2 years (read as its maximum term, an inference). Asserted.

2. **The protected-computer ceiling does not reach section 4.** s 11(1) names ss 3, 5, 6 and
   7 only. So a second s 4 conviction on a bank's computer stays at $50,000 / 10 years, while
   a s 3 offence on the same computer can reach $100,000 / 20 years. Asserted.

3. **Damage overrides a repeat conviction, and s 11 overrides damage.** ss 3(2), 5(2), 6(2),
   7(2) give $50,000 / 7 years to "a person convicted" where damage is caused, with no
   first/second split; s 11 applies "in lieu of the punishment prescribed in those sections".
   Damage counts only from $10,000 of loss within a year of the offence, or harm to medical
   care, life or public safety: $9,999 is not damage, $10,000 is. Asserted.

4. **Handing over your Singpass is presumed guilty.** s 8A(3): a user who discloses for any
   gain, knowing it is likely to cause wrongful loss, or without taking reasonable steps to
   find out who and where the recipient is, "is presumed, until the contrary is proved" to have
   had reasonable grounds to believe it was for an offence. s 8A(4) saves disclosure for a
   lawful transaction in the user's own identity. Asserted, both ways.

5. **Singpass offences can bring caning, up to 12 strokes, but no higher repeat ceiling for
   s 8A.** ss 8A(5), 8B(5A) and 8B(5B), in force 30 December 2025. Under s 8A(5)(b) and
   s 8B(5B)(b) the individual must prove reasonable steps once the court is satisfied the
   credential was used for a scam. s 8A has no second-conviction limb; s 8B does
   ($20,000 / 5 years). Asserted.

6. **Retaining another person's Singpass credential is an offence only for an offence-related
   purpose; supplying it is an offence even with an innocent purpose if you had reason to
   believe it would be misused.** s 8B(2), (3). A face image captured through Singpass and a
   registered Singpass app are credentials; an identity card number is not (First Schedule
   para 1(2)). Asserted.

7. **The s 9 Examples hold.** Keeping a list of hacked card numbers to report it, and passing
   it on for the victim's investigation, is no offence (s 9(2), (3), Examples 1 and 2);
   supplying it knowing it is likely to be misused is. Asserted.

8. **The Act follows Singpass offences anywhere; it does not follow ss 9 and 10 on the
   computer's location alone.** s 13(3)(d) reaches ss 8A(1) and 8B(1) wherever done;
   s 13(3)(b), the computer-in-Singapore limb, lists ss 3 to 8 only. Asserted.

9. **Composition is capped at $3,000**, for offences prescribed as compoundable (s 16); the
   regulations are not deposited. A compensation order counts against the victim's civil claim
   but does not end it (s 17(2)). Asserted.

## What would need doing before this is worth anything

- The regulations under s 16(2) and any Gazette notifications under s 2(1) were not read.
- The text in force before 30 December 2025 was not deposited, so earlier conduct is not
  covered by the punishment rule.
- The elements of s 6 are not encoded.
- s 14's 12-month period is taken as a stated fact; how it is counted (the Interpretation
  Act 1965) was not read.
- No case law on "knowingly", "reasonable grounds" or "damage" was searched.
- No independent test pass, coverage table or human review.
